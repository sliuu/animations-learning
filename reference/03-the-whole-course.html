0001 · Ease-out, or nothing

Skill: choose a duration and a curve, and defend both.

  • Animation's job is to preserve cause and effect. The test for any effect: does this reduce the work of understanding what just happened, or add to it?
  • The gate — animate only to answer where did that come from, where did it go, did my click register, is this the same thing I was looking at.
  • Ease-out for entrances. Fast start, decelerating into place. cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1) is the workhorse.
  • Linear reads mechanical; ease-in on an entrance reads sluggish. Both are recognisable on sight once you've seen them side by side.
  • Duration is a property of the thing, not of taste: 120–160ms hover, 180–250ms popover, 280–360ms modal.
  • A polished page isn't using a secret library. It's applying a few rules consistently.

0002 · The four-property budget

Skill: pick the property that gets the effect without buying the pipeline.

  • 16.7ms per frame, total. The pipeline is style → layout → paint → composite, and a property costs you every stage from where it enters downward.
  • transform and opacity enter at composite — effectively free, and run on a different thread, so they survive a fully blocked main thread.
  • color, background, box-shadow, filter → paint. width, height, top, margin, padding → layout, recomputed every frame.
  • Cost is cost × frequency × count. One accordion opening once is a rounding error. The same animation on 200 cards is what kills the page.
  • The one everybody hits is height: auto. Use grid-template-rows: 0fr → 1fr, or scale a wrapper.
  • will-change is a hint with a memory cost. Add it before, remove it after — never leave it in a stylesheet permanently.

0003 · Stagger and orchestration

Skill: orchestrate a group entrance and defend its total length.

  • Two numbers only: the per-item duration, and the step between items.
  • total = duration + (n − 1) × step. Compute it before shipping.
  • overlap = step ÷ duration. Keep it ≤ 0.35 or the group reads as a queue instead of one gesture.
  • Overlap and total fail independently — a well-overlapped group can still take four seconds.
  • Motion captures attention before content does, so arrival order becomes reading order. Stagger is therefore a claim about priority; make it deliberately.
  • Per-item stagger breaks past roughly ten items. Stagger by batch or by row instead.
  • The pattern: --i on each element, transition-delay: calc(var(--i) * 60ms).

0004 · The scroll reveal

Skill: choose trigger or scrub, and place the fire point.

  • Two unrelated techniques share one name. A trigger fires when an element crosses a line and then plays at its own speed. A scrub makes progress a function of scroll position.
  • If the element carries meaning, trigger it. Scrub only what is legible at any arbitrary percentage — backgrounds, progress bars, a drifting image.
  • Fire point: about 10–20% before the element's top reaches the viewport bottom. rootMargin: '0px 0px -15% 0px'.
  • The cardinal sin: content that only JavaScript can unhide. Ship the visible state and let the script opt into hiding it.
  • Stagger by batch, not by global index, or item 40 waits two and a half seconds for nothing.
  • CSS-only version: animation-timeline: view() — which is a scrub, so it inherits every scrub caveat above.

0005 · Exits

Skill: make a thing leave faster than it arrived, and survive its own removal.

  • An entrance spends time building understanding. An exit confirms a decision the reader already made, which needs far less of it.
  • Ratio 0.5–0.65. 320ms in, 180ms out.
  • The curve flips: ease-in out. An ease-out exit lingers at near-zero opacity, which is the worst possible place to spend time.
  • Direction: back toward where it came from, or straight away. Never mirrored for symmetry's own sake.
  • The engineering problem — the element must stay in the DOM until the exit finishes. transitionend, or your framework's presence API.
  • display and content-visibility are discrete properties: transition-behavior: allow-discrete, plus @starting-style for the entrance.
  • Interruption: reverse from the current computed value, never restart from the end state.

0006 · Choosing a tool

Skill: pick the cheapest tool that can do the job, and say why.

  • A CSS transition is a rule about two states. A CSS keyframe animation is a recording you can start and stop. WAAPI is the same recording with a playhead you can hold.
  • Pick by what you need to hold onto — that's the whole decision.
  • Transitions interrupt from the current value for free. Keyframes own the property outright and snap when removed. Only WAAPI gives you pause(), reverse(), currentTime and .finished.
  • The five questions, in order, stopping at the first yes: two states? → transition. Loops, 3+ steps, or runs unprompted? → keyframes. Pause, seek, reverse, await? → el.animate(). React, and unmounting or layout-driven? → Motion. Structured sequence or scroll pinning? → GSAP.
  • Libraries save you plumbing, not judgment. Every duration, curve and property decision from lessons 0001–0005 is unchanged by the tool.

0007 · Transform-origin

Skill: anchor a scaling element to the thing it came from.

  • transform-origin is the fixed point — the one place in the element that doesn't move.
  • Put it at the point the element came from. For an anchored element, mirror the pinned edge: a panel below and right of its trigger gets top left.
  • Get it wrong and the element drifts by hypot((1−s)·W/2, (1−s)·H/2) — which is why a wrong origin looks like the popover "landed nearby" rather than opened.
  • Never scale from 0. Start at 0.92–0.96, or the text is illegible and it reads as a zoom rather than an appearance.
  • The origin belongs on the element being transformed, not on its parent.
  • It governs rotation too: centre = dial, left = door, bottom = pendulum, top = hanging tag.
  • SVG needs transform-box: fill-box before percentages mean anything.
  • Leave it at centre deliberately for centred modals and toasts — things that genuinely came from nowhere.

0008 · Springs

Skill: choose between a duration and a physics model, and tune a spring without guessing.

  • A tween is a function of time. A spring is a function of state — position and velocity.
  • Only a spring carries velocity across a mid-flight change of target. A tween produces a corner, because the new curve's opening slope comes from the curve, not from what the element was doing.
  • Three numbers: stiffness (how hard it pulls), damping (friction), mass (inertia).
  • ζ = damping ÷ (2 √(stiffness × mass)). Below 1 it bounces; at 1 it's the fastest arrival with no overshoot; above 1 it's slow for no benefit.
  • Tuning method: set ζ for bounce, then stiffness for speed.
  • Motion's bounce is 1 − ζ, clamped to [0.05, 1] — so it can't express overdamped, and it's linear in ζ rather than in what you see. bounce: 0.25 is a 2.8% overshoot, not 25%.
  • Duration is an output. Consequence: the bounciest preset is also the slowest.
  • CSS linear() is a baked spring. Overshoot survives; velocity continuity does not.
  • Don't spring a stagger (no duration to budget with), an exit, or opacity (it clamps at 1, so you get the tail without the bounce).

0009 · Reduced motion, done properly

Skill: decide, per element, what a reduced-motion user should see instead.

  • reduce is a request about distance, not time. Cutting a duration to 0.01ms moves the same 24px; it just deletes the part you could follow.
  • Take the travel away and keep the fade. A 300ms cross-fade in place is a perfectly good reduced-motion animation.
  • Three buckets. Ambient (parallax, drifting gradients, marquees) → remove. Informational (entrances, exits, toasts, reveals) → cross-fade in place at full duration. Status (spinners, progress, typing indicators) → leave alone; WCAG 2.3.3's essential-motion exception covers it.
  • Risk order: large area > parallax > zoom and spin > oscillation > distance > speed. opacity is the safe substitute for all of them — no direction, no displacement, no depth cue.
  • The famous global 0.01ms reset is a floor, not a policy: it freezes every spinner on the page via animation-iteration-count: 1. Keep it for third-party CSS; write real rules for your own.
  • Structurally, one media query is enough — set a shared --travel to 0px and every component that reads it is covered, including the ones written after you leave.
  • JavaScript gets none of this for free. matchMedia('(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)'), and listen for change — people toggle it mid-session, often because of your page.
  • Never ship a reduced path you haven't looked at. DevTools → Rendering → emulate prefers-reduced-motion.

0010 · Hover and press

Skill: time a state change to the hand that caused it, and gate it on the input device.

  • Ask who moved first. Motion that answers the user is near-instant; motion the system starts on its own gets a duration. This sits above every number in Reference 02.
  • 0.1 second is the limit for feeling you are directly manipulating the thing (Nielsen). Press-down 0–100ms; zero is a legitimate answer.
  • Down fast, up slow — 80ms in, ~180ms out. Re-declare the transition inside :active, don't just override the transform. A soft release reads as physical without a spring.
  • At 80ms every easing curve looks the same. The one place in the course where the curve doesn't matter.
  • Press depth is a displacement, not a ratio: (1 − scale) × width ÷ 2. Keep it in the 2–4px band, so 0.96 on a 96px button and ~0.985 on a 320px card. Full-width things darken instead.
  • There is no hover on touch — no pointerleave is ever generated, so a bare :hover latches at the tap and stays until you touch something else.
  • Gate hover decoration in @media (hover: hover). Not and (pointer: fine). Never put :active or :focus-visible inside it.
  • Nothing that is information or a control may live behind hover. It doesn't exist on a phone and it never appeared for a keyboard.
  • Hover intent is an asymmetric transition-delay: ~300ms arriving, 0ms leaving. JavaScript only for the safe-triangle submenu case.
  • Keep the press scale under reduce — it's at the safe end of every axis in 0009's risk table. Cut hover travel instead: --lift: 0px.

0011 · Layout animation

Skill: animate a layout change you didn't write, and know when to hand it to the browser.

  • FLIP — First, Last, Invert, Play. Measure, change the DOM, measure again, apply a transform that maps Last back onto First, then take it off with a transition.
  • Layout runs once, at Last. Everything after is a composite-tier transform — which is how a layout change gets animated inside 0002's budget.
  • The DOM is in its final state from Last onward. Only the pixels lie.
  • void el.offsetWidth; between Invert and Play is load-bearing. Without it the two writes coalesce, the inverted position never exists as a frame, and nothing animates — silently. The double-requestAnimationFrame is the same fix, one frame later.
  • Any layout read works: offsetWidth, offsetHeight, getBoundingClientRect(), getComputedStyle(el).transform.
  • FLIP is interruptible for free, because getBoundingClientRect() reports the painted box — transforms included — so First re-measures mid-flight.
  • It animates whatever caused the layout change; the code never knows which. Its two weak spots are resizing (a scaled box stretches its children and its border-radius) and elements that enter or leave (no First rect to come from).
  • document.startViewTransition(cb) is the browser doing all of it on two screenshots. Unnamed elements cross-fade; a shared view-transition-name across the two states makes the browser morph one into the other.
  • The name must be on the source element before the callback runs, and must be unique in the document at capture time. A duplicate is an InvalidStateError and the whole transition is abandoned — so names carry identity: card-42, never card.
  • It always captures the entire document. For the duration the page is a pair of screenshots: no scroll, no input, no interruption — only transition.skipTransition().
  • Style it with ::view-transition-group(), -old() and -new(); every timing rule from 0001 and 0002 applies to them unchanged.
  • Support: same-document is Chrome 111, Safari 18, Firefox 144 — Baseline as of October 2025. Cross-document (@view-transition) is Chrome 126 and Safari 18.2, and not in Firefox.
  • The gate: if the user can interrupt it, FLIP it; if it's over before they could, hand it to the browser.

0012 · Things that just appeared

Skill: animate an element that has no previous state, and pick the mechanism from whether it can be interrupted.

  • A transition needs two computed values. A just-appended element's first computed style is its final style, so no transition is ever created — and nothing is logged, because not starting is not an error.
  • Three places to keep the missing first value: @starting-style, the from block of a @keyframes, and a start class removed after a forced reflow (void el.offsetWidth).
  • @starting-style is a declarative void el.offsetWidth. Same fix as 0011's flush, spelled in CSS. It is consulted only on the element's first style update, which is also why it can do nothing for exits.
  • The gate — can she change her mind mid-entrance? A transition recomputes its start from the current on-screen value, so an interrupted entrance turns around. A keyframe animation starts a fresh timeline at its own from, so an interrupted entrance snaps to fully-arrived and leaves from there.
  • WAAPI is the exception, not a contradiction: el.animate() hands back an Animation with reverse() and playbackRate — interruptible, as long as you kept hold of the object. That is 0006's ladder.
  • Where the zero lives decides what happens when the animation doesn't. @starting-style and from fail safe: an element that never animates is a visible element. opacity: 0 in a base rule makes the content conditional on JS running, which is 0004's cardinal sin and needs 0004's .js guard.
  • Use the runtime reflow when the “before” is a number you had to measure — a height, a rect, anything CSS can't name. Otherwise prefer the declarative one.
  • display needs transition-behavior: allow-discrete. A top-layer element (popover, <dialog>) needs overlay on the same list too, or it drops out of the top layer instantly and the exit runs where nobody can see it. You may delay overlay; you may not set it.
  • getAnimations() is the instrument. 0 means nothing started — a missing first value. Non-zero means something started and it's the wrong thing.
  • @starting-style and allow-discrete are Chrome 117, Safari 17.4–17.5, Firefox 129. Transitioning overlay is Chromium-only, and degrades to a hard cut — the un-animated baseline, so no @supports gate is needed.

0013 · Reveals with clip-path

Skill: reveal something without moving it, and know before you write it whether the shape is worth a paint.

  • Two effects share the name. The window moves — the element is fully painted and at rest the whole time, and only what you may see of it changes. The content moves — the fade-and-rise, dimmer and lower than its final state for the whole flight. A mask reads as uncovering; a fade reads as arriving. A reveal called “too soft” is usually a fade where a mask was wanted, and no curve fixes it.
  • clip-path keeps what is inside the shape and does not draw the rest. The layout box is unchanged, so nothing reflows, and hit-testing follows the visible shape.
  • inset() takes distances inward from top, right, bottom, left — margin order. round 14px puts a radius on the window itself. Polygon percentages may sit outside 0–100%, which is how a diagonal edge overhangs its box.
  • A shape only interpolates against its own kind — inset to inset, circle to circle, polygon to polygon with the same vertex count. Mismatch is a discrete animation: hold, swap at 50%, hold. Nothing is logged, and getAnimations() shows a healthy animation, which is what tells you the values are wrong rather than the mechanism.
  • 0002's on credit tier: it skips layout, so never the catastrophic kind of jank, but it re-paints and the bill scales with the area painted rather than with how complicated the shape is.
  • The decision is capability, not cost. Could a rectangle have done this? Stop at the first yes: (1) is the leading edge anything but horizontal or vertical; (2) does the window need more than one moving edge; (3) must the content stay perfectly still and the radius stay put; (4) is the element one you can't wrap.
  • The three builds of one wipe: clip-path on the element; a clipping wrapper that travels with the content counter-transformed back (what a split-text line mask is); a static clipping wrapper with the content sliding behind it (the default, and correctly so).
  • Reduced motion: a wipe is a large area changing luminance on a schedule, not a translate. Replace it with an 80–120ms opacity fade or with nothing. Shortening the sweep to 150ms only makes it faster to look at.

0014 · Reading a Performance recording

Skill: find a long frame, follow its pipeline, and choose a mechanism-led fix.

  • A recording is evidence, not a score. Start with a long frame, then inspect the work nested inside it.
  • Layout recalculates geometry; Paint fills pixels; Composite assembles painted layers.
  • Say what changed, how much changed, and which stage paid before proposing a remedy.
  • Repeat recordings across cold/warm cache and the slowest part of the gesture; machine and viewport change the numbers.

The design track

The engineering track is organised by mechanism. This one is organised by recognisable patterns and the words needed to review them. Its three families are product UI, expressive pages, and direct manipulation.

D001 · The map, and the words for it

Skill: name any effect, say its family, and say what it promises.

  • Nouns name the pattern, verbs name what the motion does, and adjectives diagnose how it feels.
  • Product UI values consistency and speed; expressive pages value pacing and restraint; direct manipulation values 1:1 tracking and what happens on release.
  • A convention is load-bearing when breaking it changes the promise, even if the resting frame still looks correct.
  • The review sentence: this is a [noun]; it [verb]s so the reader understands [promise].

D002 · Where things live when they're off screen

Skill: answer “fade or slide?” without discussing fading or sliding.

  • Choose the absent element's home; the entrance and exit motion are consequences of that spatial claim.
  • An anchored thing returns to its trigger or edge. A centred modal can come from nowhere. A toast belongs just beyond the edge it enters from.
  • Fade is not a neutral fallback. It says the thing stopped existing in place.
  • Exit direction confirms the same spatial model the entrance established.

D003 · Motion is hierarchy

Skill: say what an animation ranks, not whether you like it.

  • Motion is a hierarchy channel beside size, weight, colour and space — and it wins against all of them while it is active.
  • Animating an element promotes it, whether or not promotion was intended.
  • Animate everything and the channel saturates; source order becomes the leftover ranking.
  • Motion cannot repair hierarchy. When it ends, the layout hands the old ranking back.

D004 · The button

Skill: review a control by what it promises, state by state.

  • A button has seven designed states: rest, hover, focus, press, pending, success/error, and disabled. A mock usually shows one.
  • Missing feedback does not break the action; it breaks the promise that the action heard you.
  • Disabled is an error message with the text removed. Prefer a live control that spends the press explaining and focusing what is missing.
  • Hold-to-confirm is a state the reader occupies. Show progress, and make release snap back faster than the hold that earned it.

D005 · Things you drag

Skill: review a drag by its pickup and release, not its middle.

  • Nothing under the finger gets a transition. While held, only the hand may claim where the object is.
  • The five moments are lift, ghost, gap, settle, snap-back.
  • Ghost preserves the origin; gap promises the landing. They are different pieces of evidence.
  • The keyboard path is part of the interaction, not an accessibility footnote.

D006 · Decks, and what the dots promise

Skill: review a deck by what its indicator promises and whether it can keep it.

  • An indicator must answer where am I and how much is left. Dots do both at small counts, counters stay exact, bars preserve proportion.
  • Choose for the largest count the deck will ever hold, not the count in the mock.
  • A peek is the content-level affordance that says the row continues.
  • Indicators imply pages, and pages imply snapping. Free-scroll belongs to continuous strips.
  • Autoplay earns its place only when content fits the interval, pauses on hover/focus, stops permanently on interaction, and respects reduced motion.

D007 · Scroll as a timeline

Skill: review a scroll system by who owns each frame, how much distance a beat costs, and whether the page keeps the native scroll contract.

  • Trigger when the middle frame is merely unfinished; scrub when every intermediate frame explains something valid.
  • Distance is duration. Price each beat in viewport-heights, then ask whether the idea is worth the travel it charges.
  • Pin the visual that must remain constant for several changes to be compared. If nothing must remain constant, restore normal flow.
  • Parallax is a claim of depth. Different rates are justified only by a real foreground and background relationship.
  • A horizontal section maps vertical travel onto a sideways stage; keep vertical progress, resting states, and the exit legible.
  • Scroll-jacking rewrites distance, direction, or response. Catch-up can be perfectly fluid and still feel laggy because the viewport trails the position the hand requested.
  • Reduced motion flattens depth and restores content to normal flow; it never strands the story in a frozen first frame.

D008 · Text that arrives

Skill: choose split granularity, reveal style, and stagger from the reading unit.

  • Split is hierarchy made in time: lines preserve body-copy reading; words suit short headlines; characters suit one hero word.
  • Mask reads as uncovering; fade-up reads as arriving. Compare them simultaneously before choosing.
  • More units multiply the waiting. A tiny per-unit delay can still make a paragraph feel like a queue.
  • Responsive line splitting must re-measure, preserve word wrapping, and keep the original sentence accessible.

D009 · Loading, waiting, and optimism

Skill: pick a waiting treatment from the length of the wait, and defend it.

  • Two facts decide the treatment: how long the wait is, and whether the length is knowable. Everything else is taste.
  • A determinate indicator is a checkable promise. Don't make one you can't keep.
  • A skeleton promises the shape of what's coming, so it lies on an empty state.
  • Delay the loader past the common case, or most readers get a flash of loading that reads as a glitch.
  • Optimism is a bet on the failure being cheap, reversible, and visible where the reader is still standing.

D010 · Navigation and page transitions

Skill: choose a transition model from the relationship between two screens, and make the way back mean something.

  • Four models, four claims: cut says nothing, cross-fade says different, push says direction, shared element says this one.
  • Back is not forward. One code path means one animation unless someone insists; press back and describe what you were told.
  • Orientation is bought by what doesn't take part. Persistent chrome is layout, not motion, so it survives everything.
  • Ask what the smallest region that has to change is. Most navigations change less than the implementation does.
  • Cold entry test: load the destination with no history. Anything the transition was the only source of has gone.
