Lesson D011 · 20 minutes

The motion budget

Every animation on a screen is competing with every other animation on the same screen, for something the reader has a fixed amount of. This is the lesson about saying no.

Skill: answer "should this animate?" with a reason, and price an animation by how often it plays ← Lesson D010

D010 ended on a claim: a transition is a sentence about how two screens are related. Here is what that claim costs. If a transition is a sentence, then a screen with eight animations on it is eight people talking, and the reader can follow one. Motion is the loudest thing a design has, and loudness does not add up — it divides.

Say it out loud

Motion budget — how much movement a screen can spend before the reader can no longer tell which movement mattered.
Signal — motion that reports something that actually changed.
Noise — motion that reports nothing, at the same volume.
Busy — too many claims at once. A failure of quantity.
Expensive — too much cost per instance: too long, too often. A failure of price.
Gratuitous — motion with no referent at all. It is not reporting, guiding or confirming anything.
Repetition tax — the length of an animation multiplied by how many times a day it plays.
A principled no — declining an animation with a reason the other person can answer, rather than with taste.

Below is a dashboard and one refresh. Two settings: what actually changed, and what you allowed to move about it.

Try this, in order
  1. Leave both settings alone and press Refresh the data. One figure changed and one figure moved. Note how quickly your eye found it.
  2. Set What is allowed to move to everything and refresh again. Now find the figure that changed.
  3. Set it to nothing and refresh twice. Say what the screen just did to you.
  4. Now set What happened to the whole page reloaded, leaving the budget at nothing, then walk it back up through one thing and the data.
  5. Find the setting that is right for each event. There is one for each, and they are different.

The budget is attention, not milliseconds

The reason to have a budget is not that animation is expensive to run. It is that the reader has one visual system, it is extremely good at detecting movement, and it will look at whatever moves whether or not that was useful. You do not get to choose whether motion takes attention. You only get to choose what it takes it for.

That is why the lab counts three things rather than one. Signal is motion on something that changed. Noise is motion on something that did not. And the third count, the one nobody puts in a review, is the change that happened with no motion at all — the figure that quietly became a different figure while the reader was looking at the other side of the screen.

Set the budget to everything with one figure changing and look at what has happened to the signal. Nothing was removed. The one honest animation is still there, playing exactly as it did before, and it has become useless — because a highlight is not a property of the thing highlighted, it is a property of the difference between that thing and everything else. Seven animations did not dilute the eighth by a bit. They cancelled it.

This is what busy means, and it is worth being precise about it, because it gets used as a synonym for "too much" and it is not one. A screen is busy when it makes more claims than the event deserved. One long, heavy, indulgent animation on the one thing that changed is not busy. Eight two-hundred-millisecond fades on a screen where one number moved is busy, and it is busy at any duration you like.

Which is also why "less is more" is the wrong lesson to take from that lab, and why the second setting exists. Switch the event to the whole page reloaded and put the budget back to one thing: now one panel is animating and four panels changed underneath it in silence. The motion is under-reporting. It points at one figure and implies the others are as they were, and the reader believes it, because that is what a highlight means.

The right amount of motion is the size of the event. That is the whole rule, and it explains both failures with one sentence. Eight animations for a one-figure change is a lie in one direction. One animation for an eight-panel change is a lie in the other. The budget is not a number you set once for the product; it is set fresh by every event, and the only question is whether the motion is telling the reader the truth about how much just happened.

Busy and expensive are different failures

These two words get used interchangeably in reviews and they name opposite problems, which means half of all animation feedback is aimed at the wrong thing. It is worth being able to separate them out loud, because the fix for one does nothing at all for the other.

Busy is a failure of quantity: too many things claiming attention in the same second. You can have a busy screen where every individual animation is short, cheap, tasteful and defensible. The fix is subtraction — decide which one thing is the news and let the rest hold still.

Expensive is a failure of price: one thing costs too much. Too long, too often, or too heavy for the machine it is running on. A single animation can make an entire product feel slow, and no amount of removing other animations will help, because there were not any others. The fix is to make that one cheaper.

So the diagnostic question in a review is: how many things are moving? If the answer is "eight", you have a busy problem and you are looking for things to cut. If the answer is "one", you have an expensive problem and cutting is not available — you are looking at the length, the frequency, and what it is animating.

If you have read 0002: that lesson prices the third kind of expensive — the machine's bill, where animating a layout property costs orders of magnitude more than animating a transform. Everything here is about the reader's bill instead. The two are independent: a design can be perfectly cheap to render and still ruinously busy.

Go and look the GOV.UK accordion — a real, interactive component from a system a government uses to build services everybody has to use. Open and close a few sections, press show all, and notice that the height change is the entire animation budget of the component. Then walk on through ten more from the list beside it and keep counting. The harder half is this: name the three interactions where you noticed the absence and felt worse for it, and the three where you did not notice at all. That ratio is the argument for a budget, made by someone who set theirs near zero. Checked 2026-09-06. Sites get redesigned; if this one has, the exercise is still to count what moves.

Multiply by how often

Here is the arithmetic nobody does. An animation's cost is not its duration. It is its duration times the number of times the reader will see it, and those two numbers are decided in different rooms — the duration while everyone is looking at a hero, the frequency by whoever picks which control it gets applied to, six weeks later.

Try this, in order
  1. Press Use it six times and watch the left frame once, then the right one for the rest. Count how many of the six you were still interested in.
  2. Set How long it takes to 140ms and press it again. The panel is now instant — and look at what the same setting did to the arrival on the left.
  3. Put it back to 600ms and set How often you open it to 200× a day. Read the number in the corner out loud.
  4. Find the pair of settings you would actually ship, and notice that it is not one duration for both frames.

Both frames run the identical animation. Same length, same easing, same everything — the only difference is how often it happens, and at 600 milliseconds that difference is roughly a hundred to one. This is the repetition tax, and it has three consequences worth taking away separately.

The first is that a duration cannot be judged without its frequency. When someone shows you a 600ms transition in isolation and asks whether it feels good, the honest answer is a question: how many times a day? Six hundred milliseconds is generous and lovely on a page that arrives once. On the panel you open forty times it is eight seconds of your day spent watching a rectangle travel, and you will have started resenting it by Thursday.

The second is that frequency runs the other way too. Watch the left frame at 140ms: the arrival is gone. There was no shortage there — one play, no repetition, nothing to economise on — and the saving bought nothing except a page that appears with a snap. Short is not a virtue. It is the correct answer to a specific question, which is "how often?", and when the answer is "once", generosity is free.

The third is the awkward one for a design system, and it is the reason this lesson sits where it does in the track: a single duration token cannot be right everywhere. The same 300ms that makes a modal feel considered makes a hover state feel broken, and the difference between them is not aesthetic, it is arithmetic. A system needs at least a fast token and a considered one, with the rule for choosing between them written down as a frequency question rather than as a size question.

If you have read 0001: that lesson argues durations from the distance travelled and the weight implied. This is the multiplier on top of it — the same 300ms correctly chosen there gets a completely different verdict once you know it plays two hundred times a day.

Go and look Excalidraw — open it and use it for two minutes: draw a few boxes, select them, move them, open the style panel, undo a dozen times. It is a tool people keep open all day, and almost nothing in it animates. Count the frames you wait for. Then find the one or two places where they did spend — and ask what those places have in common, which is that they are the ones you do once rather than the ones you do all afternoon.

Idle motion is a standing charge

Everything so far has been motion caused by an event: something happened, and the screen said so. Idle motion is the other kind — the pulsing "live" dot, the looping gradient, the shimmering banner, the marquee of logos, the icon that breathes. Nothing happened. It is moving because it was built to move.

Price it the way you priced everything else and it is the most expensive motion on the page, because its frequency is always. It is spending attention in every second the screen is open, including all the seconds when something real is trying to be reported. A pulsing dot next to a status that has been the same for six hours is competing, at full volume, with the alert you actually need the reader to see.

The honest test for idle motion is: what is it telling me that I could not learn by looking? A live indicator that pulses when data is genuinely arriving is reporting something, and the pulse is the report. A live indicator that pulses on a timer is decoration wearing the costume of information, and it is the one to argue about, because it costs the same as the real thing and says nothing.

There is one legitimate use worth protecting: idle motion as a promise that the system is alive. Progress that cannot be measured, a connection that is being held open, a long job whose only honest statement is "still working". That is a real thing to say, and continuous motion is the natural way to say it. The distinction is whether stopping it would be a lie.

If you have read D009: this is the same argument as the indeterminate spinner, one step out. A spinner earns its continuous motion by being tied to a request that is genuinely in flight; the moment it plays on a timer instead, it has become idle motion with a job title.

How to say no

Most animation gets built because nobody had a way to decline it that was not "I don't like it", and taste is the one argument that cannot be answered, so it loses to whoever is more senior. A principled no is a no with a question in it. Here are the four that do the most work, in the order they are worth asking.

What does this tell the reader that the layout does not? If the answer is nothing, the motion is decoration — which is allowed, but it must then be priced as decoration and spent from what is left after the reporting is done. If the answer is something, ask the next question, which is whether that something should have been in the layout to begin with.

How many times a day will this play? Not "is it fast", but "how often". This is the question that turns an argument about taste into an argument about arithmetic, and the arithmetic is checkable. It also converts a lot of noes into "yes, at a third of that length", which is a much easier thing for everyone to agree to.

What else is claiming attention in the same second? An animation is never reviewed against the thing it is competing with, because in the review it is on a slide by itself. Ask what else moves on that screen in the same moment, and count. If the answer is more than two, the design question is no longer about this animation.

What happens to this screen when the motion does not play? Slow device, dropped frames, reduced motion, a reader looking away. If the screen stops making sense, the motion was carrying information and the information has to move somewhere sturdier before the animation can be shipped at all.

Four questions, and none of them is "do you like it". That is the point. Every one can be answered with evidence, which means the conversation ends in a decision rather than in seniority.

When motion is off

Turn reduced motion on and both labs go quiet, and each one says something different about it. The second one gets simpler: that reader's repetition tax is zero, and the panel still opens, still lands where it should, and is still perfectly usable. That is a useful thing to notice — if an interaction only makes sense once the animation plays, the animation was not the problem.

The first lab is the interesting one. With motion off, the figure that changed keeps a static accent flag instead of a movement, and it has to, because otherwise the change is simply not reported to that reader at all. A budget of zero is exactly the condition every screen should be designed to survive, and the way to survive it is to have a second channel — colour, a delta, a count, a position — for anything the motion was doing real work to say.

Which is the same rule as the last lesson's, arriving from a different direction: motion may reinforce what the screen says. It may not be the only place the screen says it. The budget makes that concrete, because a screen designed to a budget has less to lose when the budget goes to zero.

The CSS behind it

Show the mechanism
/* Two tokens, not one. The rule for choosing between them is a
   frequency question, so write the frequency into the name. */
:root {
  --motion-quick: 140ms;   /* things touched all day: hovers, panels, rows */
  --motion-considered: 420ms; /* things met once: arrivals, first loads */
  --ease-out-strong: cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1);
}

/* Signal: the element that changed announces itself. One rule,
   applied to whatever actually changed, rather than a rule per
   panel that fires whether or not it has news. */
[data-changed] {
  animation: report var(--motion-considered) var(--ease-out-strong);
}
@keyframes report {
  from { opacity: 0; transform: translateY(0.35rem); }
}

/* Idle motion, gated on the thing being genuinely live rather
   than on the component existing. If stopping it would not be a
   lie, it should not be running. */
.status-dot[data-live="true"] {
  animation: pulse 2s ease-out infinite;
}

/* Budget zero. Note that the flag is not a shorter animation —
   it is a different channel, because the reader still has to be
   told what changed. */
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  [data-changed] { animation: none; color: var(--accent); }
  .status-dot[data-live="true"] { animation: none; }
}

Check yourself

A dashboard polls every thirty seconds. On each poll, every card fades and rises in over 400ms. In a typical poll, one figure has changed.

The tell is that the motion is driven by the render rather than by the event. A highlight only works as a difference between the highlighted thing and everything else, so highlighting everything is not a smaller version of highlighting one thing — it is the same as highlighting nothing. Shortening the fade would keep the defect and make it faster.

The same 500ms drawer animation is used for the onboarding tour, seen once, and for the filter panel, opened around fifty times a session. Someone proposes shortening both to 200ms for consistency.

Consistency is worth having between things that are the same, and these two are not: one is a first impression and the other is a control. This is exactly the case a system handles with two duration tokens and a written rule for choosing between them, where the rule is a frequency question rather than a size question.

A status panel has a small dot that pulses continuously, on a two-second loop, whether or not anything is happening. A designer wants it removed. What is the strongest argument?

The other three are true and none of them is the design argument. Idle motion is the most expensive kind because its frequency is "always", and the cost lands on exactly the moments you most need the reader's attention elsewhere. If the dot pulsed only when data was genuinely arriving, it would be reporting something and would be worth its price.

Testers say a product "feels heavy" when the sidebar opens. The sidebar is the only thing that animates on that screen: 450ms, animating its width and a large drop shadow.

Counting the moving things separates the two failures immediately. At one moving thing, subtraction is not available and the whole fix is in the price: shorten it, and animate a transform rather than a width and a shadow, which is the machine's half of the bill. "Heavy" is vague, but it is a report of something real, and the count tells you which real thing.

A stakeholder asks for an animated illustration on the checkout page. You think it is a bad idea. What is the most useful thing to say?

A principled no is a no with a question in it, and all three parts of that question are answerable with evidence rather than with seniority. It also frequently turns into a better yes: on a checkout page the answer to "what else needs attention in that second" is usually "the total and the button", which gets you to a smaller, quieter illustration rather than to an argument.

A reader switches the dashboard from last week to last quarter. Every figure, every chart and every row is genuinely different. A reviewer says "too much is moving".

This is the case that stops "less is more" from becoming the takeaway. When the event really did touch everything, animating one panel would be the defect — it would imply the rest are unchanged. The remaining question is the price rather than the count: something that reloads this comprehensively is worth keeping short, because it is worth keeping repeatable.

What you can now do

Count the moving things on any screen you are shown, and use the count to say which failure you are looking at — busy needs subtraction, expensive needs a lower price, and the two get confused in almost every review. Price any animation properly, as its length times how often it plays, and refuse to judge a duration without knowing the frequency. Spot idle motion and ask what would be untrue if it stopped. And decline an animation with a question rather than with taste, which is the only kind of no that survives the room.

Ask yourself: open the product you work on, pick the screen you look at most, and count every distinct thing that can move on it. Then write, next to each one, the event that causes it and how many times a day you personally see it. The ones with no event, or with a frequency in the hundreds, are your budget review — and it will be shorter and more obvious than any opinion you could have had about them.
