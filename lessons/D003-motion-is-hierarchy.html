Lesson D003 · 16 minutes

Motion is hierarchy

Size, weight, colour and space are how a design says what matters. Motion is one more of those, and it beats every other one — so every time you animate something you have promoted it, whether or not that was the plan.

Skill: say what an animation ranks, not whether you like it ← Lesson D002

Every screen you have ever made states a ranking. The headline is bigger, the primary button is filled, the caption is small and grey. Nobody writes that ranking down — it is just what the size, weight, colour and spacing add up to.

Those are your channels, and you tune them without thinking about it. Motion is one more channel in the same set. Not a garnish applied after the ranking is settled: a ranking instrument, and the loudest one in the drawer.

Say it out loud

Channel — one of the ways a design says this matters more: size, weight, colour, space, and motion.
Promote — to raise something's rank. Turning up any channel on an element promotes it, including by accident.

That claim deserves an experiment rather than an assertion. Four tiles. Each one is promoted by exactly one static channel, and the fourth is promoted by nothing at all. Then the motion — a nine-pixel lift, the kind that passes a review without comment — gets handed to whichever tile you choose.

Try this, in order
  1. Give it to Churn, the plain tile. Press play. It is last by every channel on the screen.
  2. Now Revenue, the large one — then Signups, the coloured one.
  3. Then nobody, and look at the four tiles cold.

The winner follows the motion. Not the size, not the colour, not the weight — the motion, every time, including when the moving tile is the one losing every static comparison on the board. Nine pixels beat a typeface set at one-and-two-thirds the size.

The reason is that the static channels compete for attention and motion does not. It interrupts. You can decide not to read a headline; there is no version of looking at a screen where you decide not to notice something move. That is also why a badge that appears at the edge of your vision gets you and a bigger badge already sitting there does not.

Anything that moves outranks everything that doesn't.

If you have read the engineering track: lesson 0003 is about how to build a stagger. This is about what a stagger says. The order you pick there is a ranking whether or not you were choosing one.

So the question in a motion review is not whether the animation is nice. It is what the animation ranked. Here is one card and one knob — which element moves. The timing, the easing, the distance and the class are identical for all eight choices; open The CSS behind it and switch between them to watch that stay true.

Try this, in order
  1. Start where it opens: Export CSV. Press play, then read the line underneath.
  2. The number. Then the chart, then View report — three different cards, same layout.
  3. Everything. Watch what you look at first, then read what that means.

Three of those are marked as choices rather than mistakes, and that is not diplomacy. A card that exists to be read should promote the number; a card that exists to send you somewhere should promote the button. Both are defensible, and the useful question is never is this right but is the motion claiming the same thing the layout is claiming. When they disagree, one of them is lying and you get to decide which.

How the wrong thing gets promoted

Almost nobody chooses to make Export CSV the most important element on a revenue card. It happens the way most bad hierarchy happens: the animation was attached to whatever was easiest to attach it to. The last thing built, the thing that was already a component, the thing someone was touching that afternoon. Motion arrives on a screen the way it arrives in a codebase.

The everything setting is the other common accident, and it is worth sitting with. Six elements move, so motion has stopped ranking anything — except the first one. A stagger always promotes whatever goes first, so on that setting the word Revenue is now the lead element on a card about a number. Nobody decided that either; it is the order of the markup, promoted to a design decision.

The one to remember

“Animate the children of this container” is a hierarchy decision. It ranks them in source order, and source order is not a ranking anyone designed.

What motion can't do

Set the lab back to Export CSV, press play, and then just look at the card a second later. It is identical to the card that never moved. The promotion lasted four hundred and twenty milliseconds and then handed everything back to the layout.

Which is the trap. If the wrong element is pulling the eye in the static design, animating the right one buys you less than half a second of correctness and then returns you to the problem. Motion is a hierarchy channel that only exists while it is running — that is exactly what makes it the loudest one, and exactly what makes it useless as a repair. Hierarchy problems are fixed in size, weight, colour and space. Motion can only agree with that fix or contradict it.

If you have read the engineering track: this is the design-side reason lesson 0004 insists a scroll reveal be cheap and small. A reveal promotes whatever crosses the threshold first, so a long page of them is a ranking of the page by scroll position — which is fine, as long as you meant it.

In the review

You now have a sentence better than I don't love that animation, which is a taste claim and cannot be argued with. The replacement is a rank claim, and a rank claim can be agreed with or disagreed with by someone who has never thought about easing:

What did that just promote?

Followed, when the answer is wrong, by the version that fixes something: that animation makes the export link the most important thing on this card, and I don't think it is. Note what is missing from that sentence. No duration, no easing curve, no opinion about whether it should fade or slide. Just the ranking, which is the part the rest of the room can see too.

Three pricing cards fade up in a stagger, left to right. The plan the business wants people to pick is the middle one. What has the motion promoted?

The stagger is ranking them by position in the row, and the design is ranking them by emphasis. Those are two different rankings on one component. Starting the stagger from the recommended card costs nothing and makes them agree.

A dashboard shows a large revenue figure and a small grey last synced 2 minutes ago caption. The caption animates on every refresh, because it is the only thing that changed. Why is this a hierarchy problem?

The motion is technically honest — that really is what changed — and it is still a ranking. Frequency compounds it: something promoted once is an accent, something promoted every thirty seconds is the loudest thing in the room.

In a review someone says the hero feels flat and suggests animating the sub-headline to give it life. Why is that likely to be the wrong fix?

Motion is a channel you can only turn up briefly. Flatness is a static problem and gets a static fix; the animation is worth having afterwards, on whichever element the fixed hierarchy says is first.

A landing page animates twelve elements on load, each with a well-judged entrance. What has motion ranked?

A channel only carries information by contrast. Turned up on everything, it stops distinguishing anything — and the residue is source order, promoted to an editorial decision nobody made.

What you can now do

Look at any animated screen and name the element the motion ranked first. Say whether the layout agrees. If it doesn't, say which one you would change — and know that the answer is almost always the layout, because the layout is the one that is still there afterwards.
