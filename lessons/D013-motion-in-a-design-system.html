Lesson D013 · 30 minutes

Motion in a design system

Tokens and handoff. What a name has to say, what a Figma spec can carry, and the three kinds of thing that only ever exist once something is running.

Skill: turn a motion decision into something a team can reuse without you in the room ← Lesson D012

D012 left you able to look at an animation and say what is wrong with it in one sentence someone can act on. That is the skill a design review runs on, and it has a ceiling: giving the same critique twelve times, in twelve reviews, about twelve components, is not a critique problem. A token is a critique you only have to give once. The rest of this lesson is about what has to be true of that token for it to survive being reused by people who were not in the room.

Say it out loud

A motion token — a named motion decision that components read rather than restate. --motion-enter: 240ms is one; a 240 typed into a component is not.
Role naming vs. value naming — --motion-enter says what the number is for; --duration-240 says what the number is. Same number, and only one of them can be edited.
Drift — what happens when a component stops reading the token and keeps its own copy. Nothing breaks at the moment it happens, which is why it happens.
The spec — the artifact handed from design to engineering. Its job is to carry decisions, not measurements.

Below is one small screen with five moving parts and three durations. Turn a duration and watch how much of the screen answers; then switch the naming knob, which changes nothing on the stage and everything about whether the set can be edited a year from now.

What a token actually buys

The fan-out is the part everyone demonstrates. One value moves and five components restyle, and that is real, and it is not the reason to do this. A find-and-replace would also have moved five numbers. What you are buying is that the argument about 240 versus 400 happens once, in one place, instead of in every component review for the next year.

Think about what the alternative actually costs. Without a set, every new panel arrives at a review with its own duration, chosen by whoever built it, and that duration is a fresh question: is it right? Compared to what? The reviewer has to reconstruct, from memory, how fast the other panels are — and memory is a bad instrument for a difference of eighty milliseconds. The conversation is unwinnable in both directions, so it usually ends in "looks fine" and the product gets one more slightly-different number in it.

With a set, the same review is a much smaller question: which token is this, and does the component's job match the token's job? That question has an answer, and the answer does not depend on anyone's memory of a different screen.

If you have read D011: this is where that lesson's two durations were heading. --motion-quick and --motion-considered were named by frequency because frequency was the deciding question, and a set is just that move made general — the name carries the rule you used, so the next person does not have to rediscover it.

The name is the whole thing

Switch the lab to value naming and retune anything. The stage does not change — the numbers are identical, the motion is identical, and nothing about the product is different. The rail is what changes: --duration-120 is now holding 200ms.

A token named for its value is a number with a nickname. It looks like a system, it lives in the same file as a system, and it fails at the one moment a system is supposed to earn its keep, which is when a decision changes. Retune it and the name is a lie that no build, no linter and no review will ever catch. The value is right and the label is wrong, and those two facts can coexist indefinitely because nothing in the toolchain knows the name was ever supposed to mean anything.

You have two ways out and both are worse than not having got here. Rename the token, which means finding every use across the codebase and the design file for the sake of a label — a refactor with no user-visible outcome, which is the first thing cut from any sprint. Or leave it, and accept that from now on the names are decoration and the only way to know what a token does is to look up its value. That second one is what almost always happens, and it is the moment the set quietly stops being a system.

Now the same argument from the other end. The lab asks you where a brand-new tooltip should get its duration. Under role naming, the rail answers it: a tooltip arrives in place, so it takes the arriving duration — and you can pick the right token without knowing what any of the numbers are. Under value naming there is nothing to answer with: the only moves available are to guess, or to copy whatever the nearest component happened to use. That copy is the mechanism by which a token set turns back into a pile of numbers, one plausible decision at a time.

The test

Hand the token list to someone who has never seen the product and describe a component you are about to build. If they can pick the right token from the names alone, the names are doing their job. If they ask you what the numbers are, the names are decoration and you have a spreadsheet with a stylesheet's file extension.

How many tokens

Both failure modes are common and they fail in opposite directions.

A token per component — --drawer-duration, --toast-duration, --modal-duration — is a spreadsheet. It has all the ceremony of a system and none of the leverage, because nothing is ever shared, so no change is ever made in one place. It also cannot be reasoned about: there is no way to ask whether the drawer and the modal should match, because they were never claimed to be the same kind of thing.

One token is the other failure. A single duration is a system that cannot say a modal is heavier than a hover. That difference is not a nicety — it is the hierarchy signal D003 spent a whole lesson on. Collapse it and every layer of the interface arrives at the same speed, which reads as an interface with no layers.

The right number of tokens is the number of genuinely distinct decisions, not the number of components. In practice that lands around three durations, two curves, and — if the system has any expressive surface at all — a small distance scale. Three durations because there are three recognisably different jobs: feedback on something you just touched, something arriving in place, and something covering what was there. Two curves because entering and leaving are the one asymmetry worth encoding.

If you have read 0005: the second curve is that lesson's point, promoted to a token. An exit is allowed to be faster and flatter than an entrance, and a system with a single shared curve makes that impossible to express without a local override — which is drift, arriving by the front door.

The CSS behind it
/* Three durations, named for the job rather than the number, so the
   name survives being retuned. The comment is not decoration: it is
   the rule that decides which one a new component takes. */
:root {
  --motion-state:   120ms;  /* feedback on the thing you just touched */
  --motion-enter:   240ms;  /* something arriving in place */
  --motion-overlay: 320ms;  /* something covering what was there */

  --ease-enter: cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1);   /* decelerate in */
  --ease-exit:  cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 1, 1);      /* accelerate out */
}

/* One place decides what happens for a reader who asked for less. Not
   five components each remembering separately — that is the decision
   most likely to be forgotten in the sixth. */
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  :root {
    --motion-state: 0.01ms;
    --motion-enter: 0.01ms;
    --motion-overlay: 0.01ms;
  }
}

.toast  { transition: transform var(--motion-enter) var(--ease-enter); }
.drawer { transition: transform var(--motion-overlay) var(--ease-enter); }

That last block is worth saying out loud, because it is the strongest practical argument for having a set at all. The reduced-motion rule belongs in the token set, because it is one decision rather than five. A decision spread across five components is a decision that will be right in four of them. Every audit that finds a component ignoring prefers-reduced-motion finds it in the newest one, built by someone who copied an older component that predated the rule.

Drift, and why it never looks like a problem

Turn the lab's last knob: the modal is hardcoded at 320ms and stops reading the token. Now retune the overlay duration. Four things move and one does not.

What is worth noticing is how unalarming that is. The modal still opens. It still looks fine on its own. Nothing in the build says anything, nobody's test fails, and the component works exactly as well as it did yesterday. Nothing broke, which is the problem: a detached component keeps working perfectly while the system quietly loses the ability to change it.

Drift almost never arrives as a decision to leave the system. It arrives as a deadline: the modal needed to be a little slower for one release, the token was owned by another team, and a local override was the twenty-second version of a two-week conversation. That is a reasonable trade, made under real pressure, and the cost of it is invisible for months — until someone retunes the overlay duration and one dialog in the product is subtly out of step with every other layer, and nobody can say why.

The practical defence is not discipline. It is making the override visible: a lint rule, a comment convention, a list of known exceptions that a human reads once a quarter. What matters is that the exception has a name and an owner, because an exception you can see is a decision and an exception you cannot see is just the current state of the code.

The handoff

The set is half the job. The other half is the artifact you hand over, and this is where most motion work goes wrong — not because designers write bad specs, but because a static file has a shape, and some things do not fit inside that shape at all.

Three animations below, three specs, and three different kinds of silence. The card on the right is generated from the same data that drives the demo, so it cannot quietly disagree with what is on screen — which, worth saying, is the one property a real spec never has.

Three kinds of silence

Start with what does hand over, because it is most of it. Property, distance, duration, easing, direction, stagger, and the reduced-motion rule all survive being written down. A spec that carries those seven is a good spec and most specs do not carry all seven. If you take one practical thing from this lesson, it is that the reduced-motion row belongs on the sheet, spelled out as a design decision, not left as an implementation detail for someone to remember.

The drawer is silent about state. Both panes have the same duration, the same curve, the same distance — everything the handoff contained — and the right one snaps back to fully open before it closes, because it was told where the drawer starts and where it ends and nothing else. Interruption is not a missing detail, it is a missing category: a spec describes two states, and a reader can be in any of the ones between them. You cannot draw "and if she changes her mind at 40%", because there is no 40% in a static file.

The list is silent about data. Every number in that spec is correct. 200ms per row, 60ms apart, signed off against a mock with five rows — and at forty rows the last one arrives two and a half seconds after the first. A stagger is not a duration, it is a duration multiplied by a number the design file never contained. The fix is a cap, stop staggering after row eight, and that cap is a real design decision that gets invented at build time by whoever happens to notice.

The header is worse than silent. Its spec has a Duration field and an Easing field and both are filled in, and both mean nothing. When the clock is the scrollbar there is no duration to specify: the reader sets the speed, can run it backwards, and can park it half done. That is a state no timed animation ever sits in. Park it half way and look at it: that half-condensed header has to be something a person could screenshot, and it is not on the sheet, because the sheet has two frames and this has a continuum.

A wrong number in a spec is more expensive than a blank one, because a blank gets a question and a number gets built. The 300ms in that field will become a 300ms transition layered on top of the scroll, fighting it, and the resulting lag will be reported as a performance bug six weeks later.

Say it out loud

State silence — the spec describes two frames and the reader can be anywhere between them. Interruption, reversal, re-trigger.
Data silence — the spec is per-item and production sets the item count. Staggers, lists, grids, anything where n comes from a server.
Driver silence — the animation's clock is not a clock. Scroll, drag, pointer position; a duration field here is not blank, it is wrong.

What to write down

The useful reframe: a handoff's job is to carry decisions, not measurements. A measurement can be read off a prototype. A decision cannot, and it is the thing that will otherwise be made silently by whoever is holding the ticket.

So the sheet has two halves. The first is the seven fields above, filled in — and filled in with token names, not numbers, wherever a token exists, because --motion-overlay hands over the rule as well as the value while 320ms hands over only the value.

The second half is a short list of sentences, and these are the ones that get left off:

  • What happens when it is interrupted. "Closing mid-open reverses from where it is" is one sentence and it is the entire difference between the two panes above.
  • What happens at the extremes of the data. One row, forty rows, zero rows. Name the cap.
  • What happens for a reader who asked for less motion. Not "respect reduced motion" — say what the interaction does instead.
  • What is allowed to be dropped under pressure. If the animation has to go, what is the interface without it? This one is a gift to the engineer.

That last one is the D011 test written into the handoff. If the interface only makes sense once the animation plays, the animation was never the problem — and finding that out during the handoff is enormously cheaper than finding it out from a bug report about a reader who turned motion off.

The parts that only exist in code

Some things are not being withheld from the spec. They genuinely do not exist until something runs, and the honest move is to mark them as such rather than to invent a number.

Springs are the clearest case. A spring has no duration — it has stiffness, damping and a starting velocity, and that velocity comes from the reader's finger. Two people can flick the same card and get two different settle times, both correct. A spec can say "springy, and it should feel like it carries the reader's momentum"; it cannot say 400ms, and a spec that says 400ms has converted a physical system into a timed one, which is a different design.

If you have read 0008: this is why that lesson insisted the spring's parameters are not a curve in disguise. The reason it matters at handoff is that the one field a spec most wants to fill in — duration — is the one field a spring does not have.

Interruption is the second. Reversal, re-triggering, what happens when three toasts arrive at once — all of it is state machinery, and the design decision is which behaviour is correct, not how it is implemented. Write the sentence; leave the implementation alone.

The third is anything counted at runtime: list lengths, grid sizes, how many items are on screen. And the fourth is performance. Whether an animation holds sixty frames on the oldest device you support is not knowable from a design file, and it is the one item on this list where the right answer might be to change the design.

Where the engineer has to invent a decision, that is where the spec should have had a sentence. Which makes the review question at the end of a handoff a simple one: what did you have to decide that I did not tell you?

What you have now

A token set is not a list of durations. It is a set of decisions already made, named so that the next person can apply them without redoing the reasoning — which is why the naming is not cosmetic, and why a name minted from a value is a system with a fuse in it.

And a spec is not a description of an animation. It is the decisions that a running interface cannot infer on its own. Most of those write down cleanly. The four that do not — state, data, driver, and the physical ones — are exactly the four that end up being decided by whoever is building it at eleven at night, which is a fine way to make a decision and a terrible way to make it consistently.

This is the last of the judgment lessons, and the design track's shape is now complete: D001 gave the words, D004 through D010 gave the patterns, D011 gave a way to say no, D012 gave a way to say what is wrong, and this one gives a way to make either of those stick after you have left the room.
