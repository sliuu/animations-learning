Lesson D008 · 18 minutes

Text that arrives

Kinetic type is not a pile of letters with a delay. Split the sentence at the level of meaning, then decide whether the reader should see a window opening or words entering the room.

Skill: choose split granularity, reveal style, and stagger from the reading unit← Lesson D007

D007 made the reader the owner of a scroll timeline. Here the reader still owns attention, but the designer owns the order: what is the first thought, what follows it, and how much waiting is worth asking for? Text arrives well when the motion makes that order easier to feel. It fails when every character becomes a tiny appointment.

Say it out loud

Kinetic type — text whose movement is part of its meaning.
Split granularity — the unit you animate: phrase, line, word, or character.
Stagger — a deliberate delay between units.
Duration — how long one unit takes to change from its first frame to rest.
Easing curve — where an animation spends that time: early, evenly, or near the end.
Mask reveal — the words stay in place while a window uncovers them.
Fade-up — the words arrive from dimmer and lower, then settle.
Reading unit — the chunk the reader can understand in one glance.

Start with the split, not the keyframes. In the lab, keep an eye on the two counters: the hero word can carry a beat per character because it is the picture. A paragraph is a sentence; making it wait for every character turns reading into a queue.

Try this, in order
  1. Play lines + together. The paragraph keeps its reading shape.
  2. Choose characters + countable. The hero gains a pulse; the paragraph shows its tax in beats and milliseconds, and the counter under it is doing arithmetic you would otherwise have to trust someone about.
  3. Try words + quick on the hero, then read the same setting on the body copy. Keep the smallest unit that changes meaning, not the smallest unit the browser can animate.
  4. Now leave the split alone and move Order to last → first, then to centre out. Nothing about the type changed and nothing about the timing changed. What changed is which unit the reader meets first — which is a ranking, and it is free.
  5. Drag the window narrower with lines selected. The line count changes on its own, because a line is not a durable unit. Hold on to that; the last section is about it.

Go and look Apple — MacBook Pro. Scroll until a big headline arrives and name its unit: whole block, line, word, or character. Then find any paragraph of ordinary body copy on the same page and name that one. They are not the same answer, and the page is a long argument for why. Do it on two more product pages and the rule stops being something you were told.

A split is a hierarchy made in time

Lines are usually the right first experiment for body copy. They preserve the sentence's shape while giving the entrance a clear cadence. Words can work for a short headline or a list whose items are genuinely separate. Characters are a special effect: one hero word, a logo-like title, or a deliberate spelling-out moment. Character-splitting ordinary prose is almost always wrong because the eye has to reconstruct words while the animation keeps changing the target.

This is not a ban on letters. It is a question of ownership: what is the reader waiting for? If the answer is “the next idea,” split by line or word. If the answer is “this word forming,” split by character. A 35ms delay can feel like a quick beat; 80ms is countable. The more units you create, the more total time you spend even when each delay looks tiny.

The split decides how many things there are. The order decides which of them the reader meets first, and that is a second, separate ranking — the lab's Order knob changes nothing but the schedule. Reading order is the default and usually the honest one: it agrees with the direction the eye was already travelling. Reversing it says the end of the phrase is the point, which is occasionally true and mostly an accident of a preset. Centre-out says the thing is unfolding rather than being read, which is why it flatters a single hero word and fights a sentence. Whichever you pick, you have told the reader what to rank first, so pick it on purpose.

Mask or fade: uncovering or arriving?

A mask reveal changes the window in front of still type. It says, “this was here; now you can see it.” A fade-up changes the type's state. It says, “this is entering your attention.” Both can be beautiful. They are not interchangeable, and a comparison needs to be simultaneous or your memory will quietly pick a winner for you.

Both panes share every setting, so the only difference between them is the promise. That is the point of running them side by side: play them one after the other and your memory will quietly award the win to whichever you saw second.

Try this, in order
  1. Press Play both at the default settings and say which pane is uncovering and which is arriving, out loud, before reading on.
  2. Set theatrical · 1000ms. The mask becomes a curtain; the fade-up becomes a slow drift. Long durations do not flatter both promises equally.
  3. Keep that duration and walk Curve from ease-out to linear to ease-in, pressing Hold at 50% time after each. Half the clock has passed every time; the verdict measures how much of the change has been spent. That gap between the two is the entire job of a curve.
  4. Push Fade-up travel to 28px. Only the right pane moves, and it now announces its arrival much more loudly. Distance is a volume knob, not a detail.
  5. Set Line delay to together, then countable · 180ms. The cadence of the group is a separate decision from the speed of one line.

Use a mask when the spatial place is stable and the reveal itself is the story: a line being uncovered, a crop opening, a title emerging from behind a surface. Use fade-up when arrival is the story: a new section, a caption gaining focus, or a sequence where the words should feel newly present. If the type should not move, do not fake its movement just because a split-text preset offers one.

Go and look Codrops’ On-Scroll Typography Animations — fifteen numbered effects on one page, most of them louder than anything you would ship. That is what makes it useful. Scroll slowly and ask two questions of each: what is the unit, and was the type always there with something moving in front of it, or did the type change state? Keep the reading intact

Runtime line splitting depends on font metrics and available width. Fonts load, windows resize, and a line that was one unit can become two. A robust implementation re-splits after those changes, preserves the original text for assistive technology, and keeps generated units out of the accessibility tree. If the effect is decorative, the sentence must remain a sentence when motion is removed.

If you have read 0013: that lesson calls this the window- versus-content decision. D008 applies the same distinction to typography, where a few pixels of travel can change whether the reader experiences “uncovering” or “entering.”

There is also a practical CSS detail: character spans can break words if they are allowed to wrap independently. Keep characters inside word-sized wrappers, or the demo teaches a broken line break instead of kinetic type. The lab does this deliberately so you can resize it without losing the thing being taught.

Reduced motion is the sentence, present

Kinetic type is close to the top of the list of things a reduced-motion reader wants spared. A split turns one calm element into dozens of small moving parts, all of them inside the thing the reader is trying to read, and the reader cannot look away from it because it is the content. A vestibular trigger is bad; a vestibular trigger you have to stare at to get the information is worse.

The accommodation is not a faster version of the same reveal. There is no honest short version of a letter-by-letter formation — at 60ms it is not a gentler effect, it is a flicker. Remove the schedule entirely and let the sentence be present. Both labs on this page do exactly that, and each says so in a comment: the words were always the content, and the split was always decoration on top of them.

What you keep is the information. If the stagger was carrying an order — this heading, then its subhead, then the button — that order still exists in the layout and in the reading direction. It was never the animation's to give. The test is simple and you can apply it to any design: turn the motion off and see whether anything you were relying on has gone. If something has, it was load-bearing motion, and it needed a second home.

The CSS behind it

Show the mechanism
/* Split only as finely as the idea changes. */
.line { overflow: hidden; }
.line > span { display: block; }
.unit { display: inline-block; }

.is-playing .unit {
  animation: arrive var(--duration) var(--curve) both;
  animation-delay: var(--delay); /* JS spends the stagger per unit. */
}

@keyframes arrive {
  from { opacity: 0; transform: translateY(14px); }
  to   { opacity: 1; transform: none; }
}

@keyframes uncover {
  from { clip-path: inset(0 0 100% 0); }
  to   { clip-path: inset(0); }
}

Check yourself

You are animating a six-line paragraph. Which split is the strongest starting point?

Lines preserve the paragraph's shape and let the reader receive one visual thought at a time. Finer splits spend attention without adding meaning.

When is character splitting most defensible?

A single hero word can make each letter part of the visual event. Ordinary prose needs words to remain easy to recognize.

What does a mask reveal promise?

A mask changes visibility, not the type's position. That reads as uncovering. Fade-up changes the content's state and reads as arrival.

You reverse a line stagger so the last line arrives first. What have you changed?

The split decides how many units there are; the order decides which one the reader meets first. That is a second ranking, it costs nothing to change, and it is worth choosing on purpose rather than accepting from a preset.

A reader has reduced motion turned on. What should your character-split hero do?

There is no honest short version of a letter-by-letter formation — at 60ms it reads as a flicker, not a gentler effect. The words were the content and the split was decoration, so the decoration goes. If the stagger was carrying an order, that order still lives in the layout.

What should a responsive split-text implementation do when the font or width changes?

Line boundaries depend on measured font metrics. Re-split after relevant changes, keep an accessible original sentence, and preserve word wrapping.

What you can now do

Look at any headline that moves and name its unit in one word: block, line, word, character. Say whether the reader is being shown an uncovering or an arrival, and point at the thing that tells you — still type behind a moving window, or type that is itself dim and low. Price the split: units times delay, plus one duration, is the wait you are asking for, and you can say that number out loud before anyone builds it. Name the ranking the order implies and check it is the ranking you meant. Then turn the motion off and confirm the sentence is still a sentence and the order is still legible without it. When someone offers you a preset, you now have four questions to ask it, and “character-by-character, because it looks expensive” is not an answer to any of them.

Ask yourself: what is the smallest unit of meaning in the real page you want to animate? Bring one headline and one paragraph next time; we can choose their splits before choosing a library.
