Lesson 0002 · 18 minutes

The four-property budget

Two properties are free. Two are on credit. Everything else you pay for out of the frame budget, sixty times a second.

Skill: pick the property that gets the effect without buying the pipeline ← Lesson 0001

You have 16.7 milliseconds. That is the entire budget.

At 60Hz the browser gets about 16.7ms to turn your CSS into pixels on the glass, and it has to do it again before the next refresh, and the one after that. Miss the deadline and the frame doesn't arrive late — it doesn't arrive. The previous frame stays up twice as long. That doubled frame is what you see as a stutter, and there's a precise vocabulary for it that I'll lay out in a moment.

Lesson 0001 was about when and how fast. This one is about what — and the surprise is that whether motion looks smooth has almost nothing to do with how much you animate. It has to do with which property, because the property decides how much work the browser has to redo on every single one of those frames.

The words, first

I'm about to use a handful of terms that get thrown around as if everyone already feels what they mean. Most of them are informal. Here is exactly what each one refers to, so you can hold me to it.

Frame. One complete image on the screen. Your monitor replaces the image at a fixed rate — 60 times a second on most displays, which is what 60Hz means. So the browser has 16.7ms (that's 1000 ÷ 60) to produce each one. That interval is the frame budget.

Dropped frame. The browser missed the deadline. Crucially the frame is not shown late — it is not shown at all. The previous image stays on screen for a second interval, so one picture is visible for 33ms instead of 16.7ms. In a moving animation, that means the object doesn't move for twice as long as it should, then jumps twice as far to catch up.

Smooth therefore has a precise meaning: frames are arriving at a regular cadence, so movement per frame is even. It is not about speed, or blur, or the easing curve. Motion can be slow and smooth, or fast and smooth. Smooth means evenly spaced.

Jank is the standard word for the visible result of dropped frames — that stall-then-jump. Hitch, stutter, chugging, choppy and stuttery are all informal words for the same thing, and I'll use "jank" from here on. If you see one of the others in this lesson, it's a slip — they all mean "frames aren't arriving on schedule."

Two more, and these are the ones the second half of the lesson depends on.

The main thread is the single queue where the browser runs your JavaScript, calculates layout, and paints pixels. One thing at a time. If your code is busy for 300ms, nothing else on that list happens for 300ms — including drawing frames.

Compositing is the last step: taking already-drawn rectangles of pixels and placing them on screen — shifting them, scaling them, fading them. Think of finished sheets of acetate being slid around on a projector. Nothing is redrawn; the existing image is just positioned differently. That's cheap enough that it happens on the GPU, on a separate thread — the compositor thread.

An animation that only needs compositing is called composited. That single word carries the whole lesson: a composited animation is handed over once and then runs somewhere else, so it keeps producing frames even when the main thread is completely stuck.

Where a property enters the pipeline

Every frame runs the same assembly line, top to bottom:

StageWhat it decidesBlast radius
StyleWhat the computed values areThe element
LayoutHow big everything is and where it sitsSiblings, ancestors, potentially the document
PaintWhat colour each pixel is, drawn into layersThe element and anything overlapping it
CompositeWhere each finished layer lands on screenThe GPU, and almost nothing else
First principle

You don't choose an animatable property for what it looks like. You choose it for where it enters the pipeline. Enter at the top and you pay for every stage beneath it — every frame, for the whole duration. Enter at the bottom and the frame is close to free.

Animating margin-left and animating translateX can look pixel-identical. One re-runs layout sixty times a second; the other re-runs nothing.

So the budget sorts itself, and it isn't a matter of taste:

TierPropertiesRuns each frame
Freetransform, opacityComposite only

Why those two are the free ones is worth understanding rather than memorising. Once an element sits on its own compositor layer, the browser has already rasterised it into a texture. Moving that texture, scaling it, rotating it, or turning down its alpha are operations the GPU performs on the finished picture. Neither property can change what any pixel is — only where it lands and how much of it you see. That is the whole reason they're cheap, and it also tells you exactly where they'll let you down. We'll get to that.

Feel the bill

Two lanes of identical squares. The bottom lane only ever animates transform and opacity — that's your control, the shape of a healthy frame. The top lane animates whatever you pick. Watch the counter, and more importantly watch the lanes.

Start on margin-left and drag the element count up, and watch the frame strip under the two lanes. Each bar is one frame, and the bar's height is how long that frame took. Even, short, green bars sitting below the dotted line is what smooth looks like. As you add elements, the top lane's bars start growing past the line and turning orange, then red — those are frames that missed the 16.7ms budget, and the top lane visibly stalls and jumps. The bottom lane keeps moving evenly the whole time.

If 400 elements doesn't faze your machine, push to 1200. The number where it breaks is your hardware; the fact that it breaks at all is the property. The control lane has no breaking point in this range, because compositing 1,500 already-drawn rectangles is precisely what GPUs were built to do.

Then work down the list. background-color and border-radius are noticeably cheaper than margin-left — layout is skipped, and layout is the expensive stage. box-shadow is the paint tier's worst offender because a blurred shadow means sampling a lot of pixels per element. And transform + opacity puts both lanes on the same animation, which is your sanity check that the lanes are otherwise identical.

The part nobody mentions

Now the demo that actually changes how you write code — and first, plainly, what the button does, because it isn't magic. Jam main thread runs this:

const end = performance.now() + 900;
while (performance.now() < end) { /* do nothing, on purpose */ }

That's a loop that spins uselessly for 900 milliseconds and refuses to return. Because JavaScript on a page gets one thread, and that loop is holding it, the browser cannot do anything else for those 900ms — it can't run other code, can't recalculate layout, can't paint. It is a deliberately stupid way to reproduce something your real app does all the time by accident.

Set the property to margin-left and press it. It counts down from three first, so you can look away from the button and watch the two lanes instead. Three things happen at the moment the banner turns red:

  • The top lane stops dead. It's animating margin-left, which needs layout, which needs the main thread. The thread is busy, so no frames.
  • The bottom lane keeps moving, perfectly evenly. It's animating transform, which was handed to the compositor thread — and that thread has nothing to do with the loop.
  • The frame counter freezes and the little green square keeps spinning. The counter is incremented by JavaScript, so it's stuck; the square is a CSS transform, so it isn't. That pair is the clearest single picture of what "two threads" means.

When it unblocks, look at the frame strip: one enormous red bar sits where roughly 54 frames should have been. The worst frame readout puts a number on it — around 900ms instead of 16.7. Drag the block duration up to 2500ms if you want it to be unmissable.

The real reason transform wins

Compositing happens on a different thread. Layout, paint, your JavaScript, and every framework render all share the main thread. A composited animation is handed to the compositor once and runs there independently — so it survives a main thread that is completely blocked.

That freeze is not a contrivance. It's a heavy React re-render, a large JSON parse, a hydration pass, an expensive IntersectionObserver callback — the ordinary stuff of real apps. A composited animation is the only kind that stays smooth while your app is busy — where "busy" means exactly what the loop above simulates. And your app is busiest at precisely the moments things are animating: route changes, modal opens, list loads.

This also explains why native scrolling stays smooth on a page whose JavaScript is struggling — scrolling is handled by the compositor too — and why a scroll handler that animates top feels awful. That one is back on the main thread, in the same queue as everything else.

“So why not transform everything?”

Because scale is not width. Transform scales the finished picture — including the text, the border radius, and the border. Both cards below travel from narrow to wide. Only one of them stays itself.

The scaled card's text smears, its corners flatten into ellipses, and its border thickens on two sides and not the others. Push scaleX to 6 and it's grotesque. This is the direct consequence of the thing that makes transform cheap: it is operating on a picture that was rasterised at the old size.

So the honest rule is not “always transform.” It is:

The rule

Use transform when the element's contents should travel with it. Moving, revealing, entering, exiting, pressing, hovering — the content is coming along for the ride, so scaling the picture is exactly right.

Reach for layout when the element's contents must re-flow. A box that genuinely becomes a different size, with text that must re-wrap inside it, is a layout change. Pretending otherwise looks broken.

The professional version of the width problem is FLIP — measure the first and last positions in JS, apply an inverting transform, then animate it away, so the layout change happens once and the motion is composited. That's how GSAP's Flip plugin and the View Transitions API both work underneath. It's a later lesson; for now, know the name and know it exists.

The one everybody hits: height

height: auto has no numeric value to interpolate, so the classic accordion either snaps or gets faked. The two-line fix most people never learn is a grid track: an fr unit interpolates, and 1fr resolves to the content's real height.

The grid version still costs layout — fr is a track size, and track sizes are layout. That is fine, and saying so is the point:

The budget is a budget, not a ban

Cost is only half the equation. The other half is cost × frequency × count. One accordion, opening once, on a small subtree, is a rounding error — pay it and get correct behaviour. The same layout animation on two hundred cards, or running on every scroll event, or on a hover that fires constantly, is what kills a page.

Same reasoning rescues box-shadow, which is the paint tier's most common trap because every card hover wants one. Animating the shadow itself re-rasterises a blur on every frame. Instead, put the shadow on a pseudo-element that already exists at full strength, and animate its opacity:

.card { position: relative; }
.card::after {
  content: "";
  position: absolute; inset: 0;
  border-radius: inherit;
  box-shadow: 0 12px 28px rgb(0 0 0 / 0.18);
  opacity: 0;
  transition: opacity 200ms ease-out;   /* free tier */
}
.card:hover::after { opacity: 1; }

The shadow is painted once, at load. The hover only changes an alpha. This trade — pre-render the expensive thing, then animate opacity between states — is one of the most reusable moves in the craft, and it works for gradients and filters too.

will-change, and how to misuse it

will-change: transform tells the browser to promote an element to its own compositor layer now, before the animation starts, so the promotion cost doesn't land on the first frame. It is a real optimisation and it is the most over-applied property in CSS.

Every promoted layer holds its own texture in GPU memory. Put will-change: transform on a grid of cards and you've allocated a texture per card, permanently, for an animation that plays for 200ms when hovered. On a phone that is how a fast page becomes a slow one.

  • Default to not using it. Browsers already promote elements they can see are animating.
  • Apply it to the handful of elements that are about to animate — on hover of a parent, say — and remove it when the animation ends.
  • Reach for it when you have measured a stutter on the first frame, not because a blog post said it makes things faster. Full details in MDN's own warning, which is unusually blunt.

Drill: verify it yourself (3 minutes)

You now have a claim that is checkable, so check it. This is the reverse-engineering muscle from lesson 0001, pointed at cost instead of easing.

  1. Open any site you admire. DevTools → ⌘⇧P → “Show Rendering”.
  2. Tick Paint flashing. Hover things, open a menu, scroll. Green rectangles are repaints. A well-built site flashes almost nothing while animating.
  3. Tick Frame Rendering Stats and scroll hard. Watch for dropped frames.
  4. Record a few seconds in the Performance panel while triggering the animation. Purple bars are Layout, green are Paint. On a good page, animation produces almost neither.

When I read the source of the page that started this course on 2026-07-29, every animated value was a y offset, a scale, or an alpha — GSAP's y: 10, y: 20, y: 24 compile to translate3d, not to top. Not one entrance animated a width, a height, or a margin. That page feels expensive partly because it never spends.

Retrieval

Close the page above. Wrong answers here are worth more than right ones obtained by scrolling.

A menu slides in using left: -300px → 0. You swap it for translateX. It looks identical. What actually changed?

left is a layout property, so every frame re-runs layout, paint and composite. transform enters at the bottom of the pipeline — the GPU moves an already-rasterised texture.

Your app blocks the main thread for 400ms rendering a list. Which animation keeps moving during the freeze?

Composited animations run on the compositor thread, independent of the main thread. Everything else — layout, paint, JS — is queued behind your blocking work, so it simply stops.

A grid of 60 cards raises a box-shadow on hover and it feels gritty. Best fix?

Animating the shadow re-rasterises a blur each frame. Paint it once on a pseudo-element and fade that in — the free tier. will-change on sixty cards allocates sixty textures and makes things worse.

An accordion needs to open to its content's real height, which you can't know in advance. Animating layout here is:

Cost × frequency × count. A single small subtree opening on demand is a rounding error, and correctness beats purity. grid-template-rows: 0fr → 1fr gets you the true height with an interpolable value.

Why does scaleX read as wrong for a box whose text must re-wrap at the new size?

Transform operates on the finished raster: text smears, radii flatten, borders thicken unevenly. The property is cheap precisely because it never re-decides what a pixel is.
