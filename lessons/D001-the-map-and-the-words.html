Lesson D001 · 20 minutes

The map, and the words for it

The first lesson of the design track. Twelve patterns you already recognise, the names for them, and a switch that breaks each one so you can hear what the name is protecting.

Skill: name any effect you see, say which family it belongs to, and say what it promises the user ← Lesson 0012

Eleven lessons got you the numbers. You can defend 180ms over 400ms and say why an exit is faster than an entrance. What you can't do yet is stand in a review and say that drawer should come from the edge it lives on, and right now it's fading in place — and end the argument rather than start one.

That's a vocabulary gap, not a technique gap. So this lesson has no new CSS in it at all.

Twelve patterns below. Each one plays, and each one has a switch that breaks the convention it depends on — never a straw man, always something real products ship. Break a few before you read another word.

Try this, in order
  1. Play Modal. Tick break it. Play it again.
  2. Same with Popover — that break is one CSS value and you will still feel it.
  3. Break them all, then play four at random.
  4. Restore all. Filter to direct manipulation and drag the Swipe card a third of the way, then let go. Break it and drag the same way.

Three families, three sets of rules

The grey chips under each pattern are the words for what you just watched; the table further down defines every one of them. First, the filter — which isn't decoration. The three families are the first useful cut in the whole subject, because a rule that is obviously right in one family is obviously wrong in the next.

Product UI is everything that appears, dismisses, and responds — modal, drawer, toast, popover. Its rule is get out of the way. These patterns are used forty times a day by someone trying to do something else, so they are judged on consistency and speed, and the highest compliment they can receive is that nobody mentioned them.

Expressive page is the landing-page family — heroes, line reveals, pinned sections, parallax. Its rule is pacing. Here the user is an audience rather than an operator: they are being walked through an argument, and motion is doing the job that paragraph breaks and subheads do in an essay. A 600ms reveal that would be intolerable on a save button is completely normal here, because nobody is waiting on it.

Direct manipulation is anything where the thing is under your finger — drag, swipe, deck, pull. Its rule is one to one, and then let go gracefully. This is the family where duration mostly stops being the unit at all: while you are dragging, the only correct number is your own hand position, and the entire design question is what happens in the half-second after you release. That is why springs exist, and it is the honest answer to why lesson 0008 felt like it was solving a problem you didn't have.

The gate

Before you argue about a duration, say which family the pattern is in. Half the motion arguments in a design review are two people applying two families' rules to the same element and both being right.

Say it out loud

Three vocabularies, doing three different jobs. The nouns are what the thing is — modal, drawer, toast, popover, hero, pinned section, deck. You have most of those already. The adjectives are how it feels, and they come at the end. The layer in the middle is the one you're missing, and it's the one that makes a review sayable.

Verbs are what the motion does. Every one of these is on screen above; the chips under each pattern tell you which cell to go back to.

Two more you will need before you meet them: morph, where one element becomes another rather than one leaving and one arriving, and shared element, where the same object persists across a change of view. Both are the subject of lesson 0011 under their engineering names.

What breaking it actually broke

Four of the twelve breaks are worth naming precisely, because in each case you already have the underlying principle from the engineering track and this is what it looks like from the other side.

The popover growing from its own middle is a wrong transform-origin, and nothing else — one value, no timing change. Lesson 0007 argued it as anchoring a scale to its source. The design sentence is shorter: the panel and the button stopped being the same gesture.

The hero arriving all at once is stagger removed. Note what it is not: it is not faster. Same duration, same easing, same four elements. It is flatter, because four things arriving together are four things competing and the eye has to pick a winner instead of being led to one.

The row that teleports is the whole of FLIP, in one felt moment. Lesson 0011 sold FLIP as a technique for animating a layout change you didn't author. Here it is as a promise instead: the gap opening is the list saying I know where this is going, and without it you have to re-read four rows to find out what you just did.

The card that dismisses on any movement removes snap-back, which is the single most under-appreciated pattern in this lesson. Snap-back is not an animation, it is a guarantee: you may begin this gesture and change your mind. Take it away and every swipe becomes a commitment, which is why the broken version feels less like a bug and more like a threat.

The pattern under all four

None of the breaks looks wrong in a still frame. Screenshot the broken popover and the correct one and they are the same image. Every one of these conventions lives entirely in time, which is exactly why they are hard to review and easy to lose — and why having a word for each of them is worth twenty minutes.

The adjectives, briefly

The third vocabulary is for diagnosis, and it is the one you already use — just imprecisely. Each of these has a specific cause, and pinning the adjective to the cause is what D012 drills. For now, the shortlist:

  • mushy — wrong easing, usually ease-in-out where an ease-out belonged. Nothing has a clear moment of arrival.
  • floaty — too long. The motion is still going after the interest has ended.
  • laggy — the response didn't answer the hand. Almost always a press or hover that took more than 100ms (0010).
  • busy — too many things moving in the same moment. No hierarchy.
  • disorienting — something moved without spatial continuity. Things appeared where nothing was, or left in a direction that means nothing.
  • unanchored — a scale or reveal that isn't tied to its source. The popover break, exactly.
  • janky — dropped frames. The only one on this list that is a performance problem rather than a design decision (0002).
  • gratuitous — technically fine, and shouldn't be there. The hardest one to say out loud and the most often correct.

Notice that seven of the eight are design faults with design fixes. "Janky" is the only one that sends you to DevTools, and it is the one that gets blamed most often — usually by someone who means "mushy" and doesn't have the word.

The habit

The rest of this track goes family by family and pattern by pattern. What makes it stick isn't the reading, it's a habit that costs nothing: when something moves, say the noun and the verb. Out loud if you're alone, in the review if you aren't.

"That's a drawer, and it's fading instead of sliding." "That's a deck with peek but no snap." "That's a hero with no stagger." Three seconds each, and the sentence is already a ticket someone can pick up.

You will be wrong sometimes, early. Being wrong with a name is enormously more useful than being right with a feeling, because a name is something another person can disagree with specifically.

A designer says the notification panel "feels unrelated to the bell icon that opened it." Which verb names what's missing?

Anchoring is the connection between a summoned panel and the thing that summoned it, and it is a single transform-origin away from being right. The adjective for the symptom is "unanchored."

A 550ms reveal is on a marketing hero and on a settings toggle. Which is the better first question?

550ms is normal pacing on a hero nobody is waiting for, and far too long on a toggle answering a hand. The number can't be judged until the family is named — which is why naming the family comes first.

Which of these is a promise to the user rather than an effect?

The other three describe what the pixels do. Snap-back describes what the interface is guaranteeing: that you may begin a gesture and abandon it. That's why removing it feels like a threat rather than a downgrade.

A carousel scrolls freely and often ends up showing half of one image and half of the next. Name it.

Peek is the deliberate sliver that says "more this way." Landing between two slides is the absence of snap — the discrete jump to the nearest valid position. Same screenshot, completely different diagnosis, which is the case for having both words.

Ask me anything about this one. The two I'd expect: where does hover fit? — it isn't in the catalogue because it's a state rather than a pattern, and 0010 already owns it. And isn't "expressive page" just a nicer word for gratuitous? — no, but the burden of proof is much higher there, and D011 is the lesson that sets it.

If a pattern you use constantly is missing from the twelve, tell me which. The catalogue is meant to become a reference sheet, and it should be the patterns you actually meet.
