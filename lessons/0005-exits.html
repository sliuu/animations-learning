Exits, and the half nobody designs

Every entrance you've built so far has a matching exit you probably got for free. Free is the problem — the default is a mirror, and a mirror is wrong.

Skill: make a thing leave faster than it arrived, and survive its own removal ← Lesson 0004

Nobody has ever complained that a dismissed dialog left too quickly.

Lesson 0001 gave you a rule with no exceptions in it: ease-out, or nothing — fast at the start, settling gently into place, because that's what physical things do when they arrive. Four lessons later it's time to say the part I left out. That rule was about arriving. Leaving is a different event with a different audience, and it wants almost the opposite treatment.

This matters more than it sounds like it should, because exits are where UI motion most often goes from "nice" to "why is this so slow." And it's the one thing you cannot fix by choosing a better curve — you have to notice it's a separate decision at all.

Why leaving is not arriving, backwards

Go back to what motion is for. An entrance has real work to do: something new is on screen, the reader doesn't know it's there, doesn't know where it came from, and doesn't know what it's related to. Motion answers all three, and it's worth spending 300ms to answer them.

An exit has almost none of that work. By the time something is leaving, the reader has already read it, already decided about it, and — usually — just clicked the thing that dismissed it. There is no comprehension left to support.

First principle

An entrance spends time to build understanding. An exit spends time to confirm a decision the reader already made — which needs far less of it.

So the exit keeps exactly one job: showing where the thing went, so it doesn't just blink out of existence. That job is worth roughly half the duration of the entrance, and sometimes none at all.

There's a perceptual asymmetry underneath this too. Waiting to see something is anticipation; waiting for something to go away is an obstruction, because whatever you want next is behind it. The same 300ms is pleasant in one direction and irritating in the other.

Three words, defined

Unmount. The moment an element is actually removed from the page — deleted from the DOM, or set to display: none. Not "faded to invisible": genuinely gone, taking up no space.

Discrete property. A property with no in-between values. opacity can be 0.5, so it's continuous and animatable. display cannot be half-none — it's either block or none, and nothing between. Historically that made it un-animatable, and that single fact is the reason exit animations were hard for fifteen years.

Interruptible. The animation can be reversed or redirected while it's still running, instead of insisting on finishing first. If you close a menu and immediately reopen it, an interruptible exit turns around from wherever it got to; a non-interruptible one makes you wait, then plays the entrance from scratch.

The ratio

Below are two copies of the same notification, opened and closed by one button. The left one uses whatever settings you choose. The right one is the naive version — its exit is an exact mirror of its entrance, which is precisely what a single transition declaration gives you if you never think about it.

Press Open both, then Close both, and watch the right panel specifically. The state label under each panel tells you which phase it's in.

The default settings are a 320ms entrance and a 180ms exit — a ratio of about 0.56. That range, roughly half to two-thirds of the entrance, is where almost every well-behaved interface lives. Drag the exit up to match the entrance and the right and left panels become identical; that's the version most UI ships with.

Then drag the exit past the entrance and watch what a 600ms dismissal feels like. Nothing is technically wrong. It just feels like the interface is arguing with you.

Exit duration 0 is a legitimate setting and the lab will tell you so. A toast that simply vanishes is not a bug. The reader's attention has already moved on, and the only thing you lose is the small courtesy of showing where it went.

The curve flips too

The ratio is the big win, but the easing matters and it's the part people miss. Recall what the two families actually do:

CurveStartsEndsReads as
ease-out Fast Slow, settling Something arriving and coming to rest
ease-in Slow Fast, accelerating Something leaving and picking up speed

An ease-out exit decelerates at the end, which means it spends its final milliseconds crawling the last few pixels while barely visible. That's the worst possible place to spend time — it's the part of the animation with the least information in it. An ease-in exit does the opposite: it starts gently, so the departure is legible, then gets out fast.

The pairing: ease-out in, ease-in out. If you want one set of values to memorise:

--enter: 320ms cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1);   /* ease-out, strong */
--exit:  180ms cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 1, 1);      /* ease-in */

Set the exit easing in the lab to one of the ease-out options and close the panels. The lab will call it out, but you should be able to feel it first: the panel hangs around at 5% opacity for a beat longer than it should.

Which way does it go?

The lab's exit direction control is a smaller decision but a real one. A panel that slid up into view can leave two ways:

  • Reverse — back down the way it came. Reads as cancelled, undone, put back. Right for a dismissal, a cancel, an escape key.
  • Continue — keeps travelling in the same direction and off. Reads as completed, sent, filed. Right for a message that was submitted or an item that moved somewhere.

Most of the time you want reverse, because most dismissals are cancellations. But if the thing genuinely went somewhere — an email sent, a card archived — continuing in that direction is doing real work: it's telling the reader where it went.

The engineering problem

Here's the part that trips everyone, and it's not about taste at all.

To animate something out, it has to still be on the page while the animation runs. But the thing that triggers the exit is usually the same thing that removes it. So the naive version is:

closeBtn.onclick = () => {
  panel.classList.add('closing');   /* start the fade-out */
  panel.remove();                   /* ...and immediately delete it */
};

That produces no animation whatsoever. The element is gone in the same frame, so there's nothing left to fade. Entrances never have this problem — the element is already there and you're just changing its style. Exits have it every single time.

The rule

The element must outlive the state change. Something has to keep it alive until the exit animation finishes, and only then unmount it.

The old answer was to do that bookkeeping yourself — add the class, listen for transitionend (or set a timer for the same duration), and remove the element in the callback:

closeBtn.onclick = () => {
  panel.classList.add('closing');
  panel.addEventListener('transitionend', () => panel.remove(), { once: true });
};

This works, and it's what the lab above does. It's also why AnimatePresence exists in Motion, and why every React animation library has some equivalent: React unmounts a component the instant its condition goes false, so the library's entire job is to hold the element in the tree a bit longer. When we get to React, that will be the one thing to understand about it — the API looks like magic, and it's really just a delayed removal.

The CSS answer

Modern CSS can now do this without any JavaScript, and it needs two features that only make sense together:

  • transition-behavior: allow-discrete — permits discrete properties like display to participate in a transition. Instead of flipping immediately, display: block → none now waits until the end of the transition. That's the "keep it alive" half.
  • @starting-style — declares the values an element should animate from the first time it appears. Without it there's no "before" for the browser to animate out of, so entrances from display: none just pop.

Why the panel is position: absolute. An earlier version of this demo left it in normal flow, and the entrance looked broken while the exit looked fine — which is a strange enough asymmetry to be worth explaining. In flow, the panel takes up space, so the instant display flips to block the button is shoved (92px, in that layout) with no animation at all. You saw a jump, then a fade. On the way out, allow-discrete holds display: block for the whole 160ms, so the same jump happens after the fade finishes, where it's much harder to notice. Same bug, hidden in one direction. Taking the panel out of flow removes it entirely — which is also what every real popover does, and why lesson 0002's warning about Layout keeps turning up in lessons that aren't about performance.

The mechanism worth taking away from that CSS is not the two new keywords — it's where you put the transition declaration. A transition on the resting rule governs the journey to that rule, so the base rule's transition is your exit and the :checked rule's transition is your entrance. That's how you get two different durations and two different curves out of plain CSS, and it works with or without the discrete-animation features.

Interruption

One more thing the lab can show you. Turn on Auto-cycle, then click Close both mid-entrance. The panels turn around from wherever they are rather than finishing first.

Support for @starting-style and transition-behavior is good in current browsers but not universal — check MDN's compatibility tables rather than trusting a number here. Degradation is graceful: an older browser shows the panel appearing and disappearing instantly, which is the un-animated baseline, not a broken page. Same principle as lesson 0004's @supports gate.

That's a property of CSS transitions you get for free: they interpolate from the current computed value, not from a fixed start. Keyframe animations don't behave this way by default, which is a real argument for transitions in interactive UI. If a user can open and close something quickly — and they can — the exit must be able to become an entrance again halfway through.

The test: click your open/close control as fast as you can. If the UI queues up animations, stutters, or gets stuck in a wrong state, the motion isn't interruptible and it will feel broken to anyone in a hurry.

When not to animate the exit

  • Anything keyboard-initiated. Escape to close means the person is in a hurry and probably not looking at the animation. Cut it short or cut it entirely.
  • High-frequency UI. Lesson 0001's frequency rule applies harder to exits: a menu you open forty times a day should close instantly.
  • Anything blocking what comes next. If a modal's exit delays the page behind it becoming usable, the exit is costing real time.
  • Error and destructive states. When someone cancels out of something worrying, get out of the way immediately.

And for reduced motion, exits are the easy case: drop the travel, shorten the fade, or remove it altogether. Unlike an entrance, an exit almost never carries information that has to survive.

A modal enters over 400ms. What's a defensible exit duration?

Half to two-thirds of the entrance is the working range. The entrance is buying comprehension; the exit is only confirming a decision the reader already made, so it needs far less time. Symmetry is the default, not a choice.

Why is ease-out the wrong curve for an exit?

Ease-out spends its final milliseconds moving very little — perfect for settling into place, useless on the way out, where those milliseconds are spent at near-zero opacity. Ease-in starts gently and accelerates away.

You add a fade-out class and then remove the element on the next line. Nothing animates. Why?

An exit animation needs the element to still exist while it plays. The element has to outlive the state change — via transitionend, a timer, allow-discrete, or a library like AnimatePresence holding it in the tree.

What does @starting-style solve?

An element coming from display: none has no previous computed style, so there's nothing to interpolate from and it pops. @starting-style supplies that "before" state. Keeping the element alive on the way out is the other feature's job — transition-behavior: allow-discrete.

A confirmation toast for "Message sent" slides up to appear. Which exit direction carries more information?

Reverse reads as cancelled or undone; continuing reads as completed and gone somewhere. The message actually was sent, so continuing matches what happened. For a dismissed or cancelled panel, reverse is the honest choice.
