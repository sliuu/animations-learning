Stagger, and the shape of an arrival

Making elements arrive one after another is trivial. Making the group read as one gesture instead of a queue is the entire craft.

Skill: orchestrate a group entrance and defend its total length ← Lesson 0002

One element arriving is a transition. Twelve elements arriving is an argument about what matters.

You can now pick a curve (0001) and pick a property that doesn't cost anything (0002). Both of those are decisions about a single element. Almost nothing on a real page animates alone — a hero has an eyebrow, a headline, a subhead, two buttons and an image, and they all want to enter at once.

Let them, and you get a flash: everything appears simultaneously, nothing is emphasised, and the eye has no entry point. It reads as a page that finished loading, not as a page that arrived.

First principle

Motion captures attention before content does. So arrival order becomes reading order — the first thing to move is the first thing read, whether or not you meant it that way.

Stagger is therefore a claim about priority. Staggering in DOM order is fine when your markup order is your priority order. It usually is. Make it a decision anyway.

There's a second effect that's easy to miss. Spreading the same total movement across time reduces how much is moving at any one instant, and simultaneous motion is most of what makes a page feel frantic. A staggered entrance is calmer than a synchronised one even though it contains exactly as much animation.

The two numbers

Everything about a group entrance falls out of two pieces of arithmetic. Both are in the readout below.

total   = duration + (n - 1) × step
overlap = step ÷ duration

Total is what the user waits. Overlap is what it feels like — and it's the one nobody says out loud. Drag things until the timeline underneath makes sense; the bars are when each item is actually moving.

  1. Pull stagger step to 0. A flash. Everything lands on one frame and the group has no direction.
  2. Push it to 260. Now watch the timeline: the bars stop touching. Each item finishes before the next starts, and you get the machine gun — a list being dealt out like cards. Total blows past two seconds.
  3. Now find the band. Somewhere around 50–80ms against a ~400ms duration, the bars overlap heavily and the eight arrivals resolve into a single wave with a direction.
The number to remember

Keep overlap ratio under about 0.35. Each item should start well before the one ahead of it has finished. Above ~0.8 the items read as separate events, and that is the difference between an entrance and a queue.

In practice that means 40–90ms steps against 300–500ms durations, and it's why you cannot pick a stagger value without knowing the duration it sits against.

Check it against the page that started this course. Measured on 2026-07-29, it uses stagger: 0.075 — 75ms — against durations of 0.2 to 0.45s. That's an overlap ratio between 0.17 on its slowest sections and 0.38 on its fastest — inside the band, with the quickest 200ms transitions sitting right at its edge, which is exactly where a short UI transition should land.

Why per-item stagger breaks

Set the lab to 20 items and leave the step at 75ms. Total: about 1.8 seconds. The last item arrives so late that a user who scrolled to it already believes the page is broken.

This is the trap in every "add a stagger" ticket: the value is chosen against a mockup with six items and shipped against a grid with forty. There are exactly two honest fixes.

1. Budget the total, derive the step

Decide the whole orchestration is allowed 600ms, then step = budget ÷ (n − 1). More items means a tighter step, and the entrance keeps its length no matter what the CMS returns.

GSAP has both behaviours and names them, which is worth knowing because you will read it in other people's source: stagger: 0.075 means 75ms each, while stagger: { amount: 0.6 } means 600ms total, divided among however many there are. Motion's stagger() is the same idea. When you see amount, someone was thinking about exactly this problem.

2. Stagger fewer things

Only the first six or eight items need to arrive in sequence; the rest can come in as one group, or not animate at all. Nobody is auditing your grid for consistency — they're looking at the top of it.

The mistake to see once

Stagger with @keyframes means animation-delay, and animation-delay has a trap that transition-delay does not. Delete the word backwards from the CSS below and replay.

Without a fill mode, an element sits in its normal state during its delay — fully visible — and then snaps to the animation's first keyframe the instant the delay ends. Five boxes appear, then blink out one by one, then rise. It looks like a bug because it is one.

backwards tells the element to adopt the first keyframe's values during the delay. both does that and holds the last keyframe afterwards, which is what you usually want.

Transitions don't have this problem: their "before" state is just the element's resting CSS, so a delayed transition simply sits there looking correct. That alone is a decent reason to prefer transitions for entrances.

Build the hero

Here's the pattern worth taking to real work: one custom property per element carrying its position in the sequence, and one line of arithmetic doing the stagger. No :nth-child chains, and the delay survives reordering the markup.

Two judgements are buried in that markup, and they're the difference between orchestration and a for-loop.

The two buttons share one index

They're one unit — a decision point, not two independent objects. Give them --i:3 and --i:4 in the playground and it immediately feels fussy: you've asserted that "Get started" matters more than "Read the docs" by a measurable 70 milliseconds, and the user's eye now travels left-to-right through a button pair it wanted to compare at a glance. Stagger between groups, not between every element.

The art moves slower, not later

It gets 560ms instead of 420ms. Larger objects that move at the same speed as small ones read as weightless — this is straight out of animation's classic weight principles, and it's why the hero image on a good marketing page always feels slightly more settled than the text above it. Give .art the same duration as everything else and it reads as a sticker.

Set --step to 240ms and the whole thing falls apart in the way you now have vocabulary for: the overlap ratio goes from 0.17 to 0.57, the total from about 840ms to 1.5s, and five elements become five events.

When not to stagger

Lesson 0001's gate applies to whole orchestrations, not just single animations, and it bites harder here because stagger multiplies the wait.

  • Menus and dropdowns. The user opened it to click something. Staggering the items delays the moment the list is readable. Fade the container, not the children.
  • Scroll reveals on long grids. People scroll faster than your stagger. Items arrive after the eye has already passed them, which reads as lag. Keep it under 40ms and cap how many participate.
  • Anything a returning user sees daily. A 900ms hero orchestration is delightful once and a tax on the fortieth visit.
  • Exits. Almost never stagger an exit. The user has decided; drawn-out departure reads as the interface arguing. If you must, reverse the order and halve every number — which is 0001's enter/exit asymmetry applied to a group.

Retrieval

Twelve cards, each animating for 400ms with a 120ms stagger step. What is wrong with it?

Total is 400 + 11 × 120 = 1720ms. The overlap ratio is 120 ÷ 400 = 0.3, which is fine — so this is a total-length failure, not an overlap one. The two numbers fail independently, and you have to check both.

You see stagger: { amount: 0.6 } in someone's GSAP. What does it mean?

amount is the total spread, divided among however many elements there are — so the entrance keeps its length as the count changes. stagger: 0.6 without the object would mean 600ms each.

Boxes with animation-delay flash visible, blink out, then animate in. The fix?

During its delay an element sits in its resting state unless a fill mode says otherwise. backwards adopts the first keyframe early; both also holds the last one after.

A dropdown's eight menu items stagger in at 40ms each. Why is this worse than fading the whole menu?

The user opened the menu to choose. Stagger postpones the moment the options can be scanned, so the motion is pure latency on an action already committed to. Animate the container.

A hero's two side-by-side buttons currently have consecutive stagger indices. Why collapse them to one?

Stagger asserts priority. A button pair is a single decision the user wants to compare at a glance, so sequencing them invents a hierarchy that isn't there. Stagger between groups, not between every element.
