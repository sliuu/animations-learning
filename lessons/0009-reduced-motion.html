Lesson 0009 · 18 minutes

Reduced motion,
done properly

Eight lessons of adding motion. This one is about taking the right parts of it away — which is a design decision, not a kill switch.

Skill: decide, per element, what a reduced-motion user should see instead ← Lesson 0008

prefers-reduced-motion: reduce is a request for less movement. It is not a request for a faster website, and those are different axes.

Somebody turned on a setting in their operating system — Reduce motion on macOS and iOS, Show animations off on Windows, Remove animations on Android. The browser forwards it to your CSS. Most of the people who set it did so because motion on a screen makes them feel physically unwell: nausea, dizziness, headache. That's vestibular — your inner ear's balance system, which takes moving imagery as evidence that your body is moving, and objects when the rest of your body says otherwise.

So this isn't a preference in the way dark mode is a preference. And the value in the spec is reduce, not none.

Try this, in order
  1. Press Loop it and leave it running — the stage keeps replaying so you can look at one thing at a time.
  2. The preference starts at reduce and the policy at the global reset. Watch the spinner next to "Syncing 3 files…" for a few seconds before reading anything.
  3. Now switch the policy to decide per kind of motion and watch the same spinner.
  4. Switch back and forth between those two and watch the hero card instead. One of them cuts, one of them fades.
  5. Drag travel distance to 80px, then set the policy back to ignore the preference.

What you just saw

That reset in the middle option isn't a straw man I built for the lab. It's this, more or less verbatim, and it is in an enormous number of stylesheets:

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  *, *::before, *::after {
    animation-duration: 0.01ms !important;
    animation-iteration-count: 1 !important;
    transition-duration: 0.01ms !important;
    scroll-behavior: auto !important;
  }
}

It gets the ambient band exactly right — that large drifting gradient is the single riskiest thing on the stage, and the reset stops it dead. Then it does two things you should not want.

It freezes the spinner. animation-iteration-count: 1 applies to every looping animation on the page, and a spinner's whole job is to loop. What's left is a static ring next to the words "Syncing 3 files…", with nothing corroborating them. The interface now claims to be working and shows no evidence of it. That's not a reduced experience, it's a broken one.

And it turns the entrances into jump cuts. A jump cut is the card appearing in its final position in a single frame, with nothing in between. Notice what 0.01ms actually did there: the card still moved 24px. It just did it too fast for you to see the path. The displacement is identical; only the smoothing is gone. For a reader who is sensitive to motion, an unexplained instant relocation is not obviously easier to take than a short smooth one — and it's harder for everyone to follow, because the thing that told you where the card came from has been deleted.

The rule this gives you

reduce is a request about distance, not about time.

Take away the travel and keep the fade. A 300ms cross-fade in place is a perfectly good reduced-motion animation. A 0.01ms 300px slide is not.

Why 0.01ms and not 0s, though? Because events. A duration of zero means some browsers never fire transitionend or animationend, and any JavaScript waiting on that callback to remove a class, unmount a node or release a focus trap simply hangs. 0.01ms is fast enough to be invisible and slow enough to still be an animation. Whoever wrote that reset knew exactly what they were doing — the mistake is treating a safety floor as a policy. Keep it at the bottom of your stylesheet to catch third-party CSS you don't control. Then write real rules for the code you do.

Three kinds of motion, three different answers

The reason a global rule can't be right is that "animation" isn't one thing. Sort every effect on a page into one of three buckets and the decision makes itself.

KindExamplesUnder reduce
Ambient
decoration		 Parallax, drifting gradients, marquees, auto-advancing carousels, floating blobs, anything that moves without being asked Remove it. Nothing is lost. This bucket is also where nearly all of the actual harm lives.
Informational
most of lessons 0001–0008		 Entrances and exits, toasts, drawers, modals, scroll reveals, layout shifts after a filter Keep the timing, drop the travel. Cross-fade in place. The message — this is new, this left, these are related — survives without displacement.
Status
the motion is the content		 Spinners, indeterminate progress, typing indicators, live audio meters, a pulsing "recording" dot Leave it alone. It's small, contained, and removing it deletes information rather than decoration.

That third row has standing, not just taste. WCAG 2.3.3, Animation from Interactions asks you to let users turn off motion triggered by interaction unless the animation is essential to the functionality. A loading indicator is the textbook essential case. Reach for that exception honestly, though: "essential" means the information is unavailable without it, not that you like the effect.

What actually causes the problem

Worth being specific, because it changes what you cut first. Roughly in descending order of risk:

RiskierSafer
Motion covering a large share of the viewport Motion confined to a small element
Parallax — planes moving at different rates, which manufactures a depth cue your balance system didn't ask for Everything moving together, or nothing moving
Scaling and zooming large elements; spinning and rotation Opacity and colour, which have no direction at all
Oscillation — bounce, wobble, anything that reverses more than once A single monotonic move that arrives and stops
Long travel distances, and high speed over that distance Short travel; a slower version of the same move
Motion the user didn't start Motion that answers a click they just made

Two things to take from that. First, opacity is the workhorse substitute — it carries the "something changed here" signal with no direction, no displacement and no depth cue, which is why "fade in place" is the default answer for the whole informational bucket. Second, none of these have a number attached. There's no threshold in the spec, no "under 20px is fine." These are heuristics from accessibility practice, so when a case is genuinely borderline, fade it.

Springs, specifically

Lesson 0008 ended by pointing at this row. Oscillation is near the top of the risk list, and a spring's whole appeal is that it oscillates.

The tempting fix is to keep the spring and set bounce: 0 (or push the damping ratio to 1). Don't. You've removed the overshoot but kept the long asymptotic tail — a spring approaches its target and never quite arrives, so what you've built is a slow, soft, oddly indefinite move that is still drifting a second later. That's a compromise nobody asked for.

Under reduce, replace the spring outright with a short tween — 120–200ms, ease-out, no displacement. In Motion that's useReducedMotion() returning a boolean you branch the whole transition on, not a parameter you soften.

How to wire it, structurally

Three idioms, and only one of them scales.

1. Override. Write full motion, then take it back inside @media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce). Reads naturally and is what everyone starts with. The failure mode is that it's opt-out per component — the day someone adds a new animated card and forgets the override, that card is unprotected and nothing tells you.

2. Opt-in. Put the motion itself inside @media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference), so the still version is the default and animation is the exception. Genuinely safer, and correspondingly annoying: every animated rule in the codebase now lives inside a media query.

3. A custom-property switch. One media query for the whole design system, and every component that reads the variable is covered the moment it's written:

:root {
  --travel: 24px;      /* how far things move */
  --dur: 380ms;
  --ease: cubic-bezier(0.23, 1, 0.32, 1);
}

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  :root { --travel: 0px; }   /* that is the entire policy for informational motion */
}

.card {
  opacity: 0;
  transform: translateY(var(--travel));
  transition: opacity var(--dur) var(--ease), transform var(--dur) var(--ease);
}
.card.in { opacity: 1; transform: none; }

When --travel goes to zero the transform animates from none to none, the opacity transition is untouched, and the card cross-fades in place at full duration — without the component knowing the preference exists. That is exactly what the lab's third policy does; the CSS panel next to the stage has been showing you this rule the whole time. Ambient and status motion still get named individually, but there are only ever a few of those, and naming them is the point.

In Tailwind this is the motion-safe: and motion-reduce: variant pair — motion-safe:translate-y-6 gives you idiom 2 per-utility, motion-reduce:translate-y-0 gives you idiom 1. Prefer motion-safe:: an unprefixed utility is unprotected, and a motion-safe: one that you forget to write just means no animation, which fails in the safe direction. Docs here.

JavaScript gets none of this for free

The media query reaches CSS. It does not reach el.animate(), or GSAP, or Motion, or your IntersectionObserver from 0004. Ask directly:

const reduce = matchMedia('(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)');

const travel = reduce.matches ? 0 : 24;

// People toggle this mid-session — often *because* your page made them queasy.
reduce.addEventListener('change', (e) => applyMotionPolicy(e.matches));

Reading .matches once at boot and caching it is the common bug. Someone hits your page, feels ill, goes to system settings, comes back — and your single-page app is still running the animations it decided on three minutes ago. The lab's live readout under the table is doing exactly this listen-and-repaint, which is why it updates if you change the real OS setting while this page is open.

Framework equivalents: Motion's useReducedMotion(), and GSAP's gsap.matchMedia(), which scopes whole timelines to a media query and reverts them cleanly when it stops matching.

Two more places CSS won't save you: scroll-behavior: smooth — a scripted smooth scroll is large-area motion the user didn't initiate, so gate it — and the scroll-driven animations from 0004. A scrubbed parallax is the single highest-risk pattern on the risk table and gets no automatic protection whatsoever.

Three ways to get this wrong

Treating it as a performance switch. Cutting durations is the reflex, and it's the wrong axis — you've made the same displacement happen faster. If you only change one number, change the distance.

Removing the animation and the element with it. This one bites hard. An element that starts at opacity: 0 and is only ever made visible by its own keyframe becomes permanently invisible the moment you write animation: none in your reduce block. The global reset happens to dodge this — 0.01ms is still an animation, so it still lands on its end state — but hand-written overrides don't. Every time you write a reduced-motion rule, actually turn the preference on and look at the page. Which brings us to:

Never testing the reduced path. In Chrome: DevTools → ⋮ → More tools → Rendering → Emulate CSS media feature prefers-reduced-motion. Set it to reduce and leave it there while you build the reduced version; you'll find the invisible elements in about ten seconds. Better still, spend a day with it on at the OS level (macOS: System Settings → Accessibility → Display → Reduce motion). Every one of those rendering emulations is worth knowing, but this is the one you'll use weekly.

The mistake this lesson exists to prevent

Shipping a reduced-motion version you have never looked at. A media query you wrote and never rendered is not accessibility work — it's the appearance of it, and it fails silently, for exactly the users least able to tolerate the failure.

What this changes about the previous eight lessons

Not much, structurally, and that's the good news. Everything you've built already separates transform from opacity because of 0002's four-property budget — and that separation turns out to be exactly the seam you cut along here. Reduced motion drops the transform and keeps the opacity. You've been writing reduced-motion-ready CSS since lesson two without knowing it.

The three things that need real re-examination: the stagger (a stagger of fades is fine; a stagger of slides is eight separate displacements), scroll scrubbing, and springs. Go back through anything you've built with those and give it the three-bucket sort.

Why does the popular global reset use 0.01ms rather than 0s?

At zero duration some browsers skip the completion event entirely, and any JS waiting on it — to remove a class, unmount a node, release a focus trap — never resumes. 0.01ms is invisible to the eye and still a real animation to the engine.

Which of these is most likely to cause a vestibular reaction?

Large-area, unprompted, and parallax — three of the top risks at once. The spinner is small, contained and informational; the card is short, small and answers a deliberate action. Area and depth cues matter far more than the presence of animation.

Under the global reset, what happens to a loading spinner, and why?

One iteration of 0.01ms, then nothing. The interface still says "Syncing" with no evidence behind it. This is the case for deciding per element rather than globally — the spinner's motion isn't decoration, it's the message.

You have one media query's worth of effort to spend on a whole design system. Where does it go?

Distance is the axis that matters, and a custom property is the only one of the three that covers components written after you left. Every element that reads --travel cross-fades in place at full duration, with no per-component work and no chance of an element being stranded invisible.

A drawer opens with a spring. What's the reduced-motion version?

Killing the bounce leaves the long asymptotic tail — soft, slow, still drifting a second later, and nobody's idea of a good time. Reduced motion is a different animation, not a detuned one. Branch on the boolean and write a short tween.
