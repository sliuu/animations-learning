Lesson D002 · 18 minutes

Where things live when they're off screen

You never choose an animation. You choose where the thing came from, and the animation is what falls out. This is the lesson that makes a motion decision arguable instead of a matter of taste.

Skill: answer “should this fade or slide?” without ever discussing fading or sliding ← Lesson D001

Should this fade or slide? is the most common question in a motion review, and it is the wrong one. It has no answer, because it is a question about the animation — and the animation is not the decision.

Here is the version that does have an answer. Where is this thing when you can't see it? A drawer is past the edge of the screen. An account menu is inside the button you clicked. A confirm dialog is nowhere at all — it did not exist a second ago. Answer that, and the motion stops being a preference. It becomes arithmetic between two rectangles.

Say it out loud

Home — where an element is when it is not on screen.
Origin — that home expressed as a point: where the motion starts.

One small app, four things it can show you, and the only knob is a place.

Try this, in order
  1. Navigation panel, home past the left edge. Open and close it. Now move its home to past the right edge and do it again.
  2. Account menu, home inside its button, then above the top edge.
  3. Confirm dialog: try all six. Two of them are defensible and you should be able to feel which before you read the line under the frame.

Nothing there was an animation choice

Open The CSS behind it and switch homes with it open. Every value in that panel is derived. The panel's resting rectangle is fixed — that is what a nav panel is — and the home supplies the other end. Subtract one from the other and you have the transform. There was no point at which anyone chose a direction, a distance, or a fade.

This is why the question is worth changing. Fade or slide is a matter of taste and the loudest person in the room wins it. Where does this live when it's closed is a question about the product, and the answer is checkable: point at the button, point at the edge, point at the nothing.

Three kinds of home

Across the twenty-four combinations you just played, the ones that worked sort into three groups — and the three groups are the vocabulary.

Retrieved. The thing has a home off screen and you are fetching it. Drawers, sheets, sliding nav, toasts. It comes from its edge and it goes back to its edge, and the round trip is the point: it was put away, not destroyed. Everything in this group has a side, and the side is not negotiable — it is wherever the product decided that thing lives.

Grown. The thing lives inside the control that opens it. Account menus, popovers, tooltips, an expanding card. There is no edge involved; the origin is another element on screen — the anchor — and the motion's whole job is to say these two things are one thing. Get the anchor wrong and you have not made it uglier, you have made it a different object.

If you have read the engineering track: this is lesson 0007 from the other side. That lesson argues transform-origin as a property with a default that is wrong most of the time. This one says the same thing as a design claim — the anchor is where the object came from, and it is a fact about the interface rather than a coefficient.

Conjured. The thing has no home. A confirm dialog was not sitting somewhere waiting; it was made for this moment and it will be unmade. Modals, alerts, and command palettes live here.

Conjured is a real answer, not the answer you give when you can't think of one. But it costs something the other two get free. A retrieved element proves it still exists by going somewhere; a grown element proves what it belongs to by staying attached. A conjured element can prove neither, so something else has to carry that weight — which is what the scrim is for. Turn on Confirm dialog in the lab and watch the dimming: the scrim is not decoration and it is not a mood. It is the thing standing in for an origin, telling you the rest of the app is still there and still yours.

Falls out of this for free

A retrieved element does not need to fade, and mostly shouldn't. The edge of the screen already hides it — a fade on top of the slide is a second answer to a question the slide has already answered, and it is what makes an entrance read as soft when you wanted it crisp.

A conjured element has no edge to hide behind, so it must fade; there is nowhere else for it to be. Look back at the CSS panel: opacity appears only for the two homes that have no edge. Nobody decided that either.

The exit is a claim about where it went

So far the round trip has been symmetrical: everything went back to the home it came from. That is a choice, and it is worth seeing broken, because the exit is where most real interfaces quietly stop making sense.

Three drawers below. Identical entrance, three ways out, all at the same time — one press.

The first one is the only one that keeps a promise. It came from the right, it went back to the right, and the screen still contains the information that the filters are over there. Nothing was said out loud; the drawer's own path said it.

The second is the one that ships most often, because a fade is the default and nobody argues with it. Watch it again and notice what it costs: the panel is not away, it is gone. That is fine for something genuinely conjured — a dialog that fades out is being honest about being unmade — and it is a small lie for something that has a home. It is why a drawer you use forty times a day can feel slightly unmoored without anyone being able to say why.

The third is the interesting failure, because it is the only one that is actively disorienting rather than merely mute. It came from the right and left on the left, so it is now on a side it has never been on. Reopen it and it comes from the right again, having apparently travelled back around. Every time a menu overlay does this, it is teaching the user a map of the interface that isn't true.

If you have read the engineering track: lesson 0005 argues that exits should be faster than entrances and use a different curve. That is about pace. This is about direction, and the two are independent — you can get the timing exactly right and still send the thing to the wrong place.

The sentence to say in a review

You now have a question that ends arguments rather than starting them. When someone shows you a panel and asks whether it should fade or slide, the reply is:

Where does this live when it's closed?

Either they can point at something — an edge, a button, or nothing — or they can't. If they can, the motion is settled and you are done. If they can't, you have found a real problem, and it was never a motion problem: nobody has decided what this thing is. That is worth fifteen minutes of the meeting and a fade is not.

And when you are the one being asked, the whole answer fits in a sentence of the same shape. It lives behind the hamburger, so it comes from the left and goes back to the left. It lives inside the avatar, so it grows out of the avatar. It doesn't live anywhere, so it's conjured and the scrim does the work. Nobody can disagree with that without disagreeing about the product, which is the only argument worth having.

A search results panel drops down from a search field at the top of the page. When it closes, it fades in place. What is wrong with that?

The entrance made a claim — I am part of this field — and the exit drops it. Anything that grows out of a control should shrink back into it, or the relationship only exists in one direction.

Why does a drawer sliding in from the left usually not need a fade, while a modal always does?

Occlusion is doing the work the fade would otherwise do. A conjured element has no occluder, so opacity is the only way it can arrive at all.

A designer says a confirm dialog should slide up from the bottom edge. What is the strongest thing you can say back?

Coming from the bottom edge is not a decoration on a dialog, it turns it into a different pattern with a different home and different dismissal rules — you can now swipe it away. Which is right on a phone and borrowed on a desktop. Naming the pattern is a stronger move than declaring the motion wrong.

A nav overlay enters from the right and exits to the left. Which adjective from D001 is the accurate complaint?

Nothing here is a performance or a timing fault; the motion could be perfect and the problem would remain. It is a spatial claim that contradicts itself, which is exactly what disorienting names.

Ask me anything about this one. Especially: bring me a real interface where you can't answer the question. Things that genuinely have no home are the interesting cases — a command palette, a notification centre, a floating video player that follows you between pages — and a few of them are open problems rather than mistakes.
