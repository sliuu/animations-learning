Lesson D006 · 16 minutes

Decks, and what the dots promise

Every carousel makes two claims before you touch it: where you are, and how much is left. Most of the design work is picking a way to make those claims that stays true when the content grows.

Skill: review a deck by what its indicator promises and whether it can keep it ← Lesson D005

A deck is a room with one wall visible. That is a strange thing to put on a page, and it only works if the room tells you honestly how big it is. When a deck fails it is almost never because the sliding was badly animated. It is because you could not tell where you were, or you never knew there was anywhere else to go.

Say it out loud

Deck — the family name for carousels, sliders and paged strips: many items, one or two visible.
Page — one resting position of the deck. Paging is moving a whole one at a time.
Peek — the sliver of the next item, deliberately left visible.
Indicator — the dots, the counter or the bar: whatever claims to say where you are.
Snap — the discrete jump to the nearest valid position when you let go.
Free scroll — no snapping; it stops wherever the momentum runs out.
Rubber-band — the resistance past the last item, instead of a dead stop.
Autoplay — the deck advancing without being asked.

Here is one deck with four decisions in it. Three of them are the ones you'd name in a review. The fourth — how many items there are — is the one that actually decides the first, and it is the one nobody revisits after launch.

Try this, in order
  1. Drag the strip. Flick it. Push past the last card and feel the resistance.
  2. Leave the indicator on dots and move the count: 4, then 12, then 40. At each one, ask yourself the two questions — where am I, and how much is left.
  3. At 40, switch the indicator to a counter, then a bar. Notice what each one answers and what each one gives up.
  4. Set the indicator to nothing and the next card to hidden. Look at it as if you had just landed on the page.
  5. Set the release to drifts and flick it a few times.

An indicator makes two promises

The two questions are not the same question, and no indicator answers both well. Dots answer both, brilliantly, up to about seven items — you take in five, and I'm on the second without counting, the way you take in the pips on a die. That is a perceptual limit, not a style preference, and it is why dots feel so right in the design file where the deck has four cards in it.

Past that limit dots do not degrade gracefully; they stop being information. Forty dots tell you there are many and put that non-answer in the exact place your eye goes for where am I. You saw it above: the active dot in a row of forty is not findable at a glance. A counter keeps both promises at any length, but you have to read it, so it never gives you the feeling of proportion. A bar gives you proportion at a glance and never tells you the count, so "one more swipe or nine" stays open.

So the review question is not are dots right here. It is how many items will this hold in a year, and then the indicator follows from the answer. A deck whose content is editorial and curated stays at five and keeps its dots. A deck fed by a query — recently viewed, in your area, more like this — will be at forty within two quarters and needs a counter or a bar from the start.

The one to remember

Pick the indicator from the largest number of items the deck will ever hold, not the number in the mock. Dots are a promise you can only keep at small counts.

The peek is the whole affordance

Set the next card to hidden again and look at the deck cold. It fills its box. It has a clean edge on the right. There is nothing about it that says it is a strip rather than a panel — and a person who does not know it scrolls will not scroll it. The deck is fully functional and completely invisible, which is the most expensive kind of broken, because it reports as working.

A peek fixes this with no words: half an inch of the next card, cut off by the edge, is an unambiguous statement that the row continues. It is doing the same job an indicator does but it is doing it in the content, which is why a deck with a good peek can often drop the indicator to a bare counter and lose nothing.

This is why the peek is not a decorative margin choice, and why "make the cards fill the width, it looks tidier" is the single most common way a working deck gets broken in a visual polish pass. If someone asks for that, the thing to say is not I like the peek. It is the peek is the only thing on screen telling anyone this scrolls.

If you have read the engineering track: this is the same argument as the one in 0004 about revealing things on scroll — the cheapest affordance is always the one made of content that is already there, rather than a widget added next to it.

Snap, or drift

Snapping is a claim about what the items are. Snap says: these are pages, they have a correct position, and one flick moves you exactly one. Free scroll says: this is a continuous strip, and stopping halfway between two things is a legitimate place to be.

You felt the mismatch above. With drifts on, a flick leaves the deck at rest with two cards half-cut at both edges, and neither of them is readable — because these cards are pages, and half a page is nothing. Free scroll is right when the items are small enough that several are visible at once and cutting one at the edge costs nothing: a row of thumbnails, a tag strip, an avatar list. It is wrong the instant a single item fills most of the window.

There is a useful test. If the deck has an indicator at all, it must snap — an indicator says you are on item three, and free scroll makes that sentence false about half the time. A deck that drifts should be indicated by its peek and nothing else.

If you have read the engineering track: the release in a snapping deck is a velocity decision, not a position one — a short fast flick should advance a page even though the finger barely moved. 0008 is the argument for why that release wants a spring or a strong ease-out rather than a linear slide.

Autoplay has to argue for itself

Everything above assumes the reader moves the deck. Autoplay is the deck moving itself, and it is worth being precise about why it is contentious, because "autoplay is bad" loses to a stakeholder who wants three messages in one hero slot and has a good reason for wanting it.

Try this, in order
  1. Read the slide. Actually read it, to the end of the sentence.
  2. Set the interval to 3s and try again.
  3. Set pointer on it to changes nothing, and try again with your cursor sitting on the text.
  4. Set after you touch it to it carries on, then click back to the slide you were reading. Wait.
  5. Now set it to stops for good and do the same thing.

The counter names a failure that is otherwise silent. Being interrupted mid-sentence does not feel like a bug while it is happening; it feels like your own failure to read fast enough. That is exactly why autoplay survives usability reviews — the person it fails does not report it, they blame themselves and leave.

Step four is the vicious one. Clicking back to a slide is an explicit statement of intent, and a deck that resumes afterwards overrules it. There is no reading of that interaction where the reader wanted the deck to keep going, which is why stop permanently on first interaction is not a nicety. It is the difference between a control and a fight.

So the position to hold in the review is not a ban. It is a set of conditions, and autoplay is fine when it meets all of them: the slides carry no text that takes longer to read than the interval — images, or three words; it pauses on hover and on keyboard focus; it stops permanently the first time anyone touches it; and it does not start at all for a reader who has asked for reduced motion. A hero of three photographs can meet every one of those. A hero of three paragraphs cannot meet the first, and that is the whole argument, in one sentence you can say out loud.

If you have read the engineering track: the reduced-motion condition is the strongest case of the argument in 0009 — an unrequested, looping, attention- grabbing movement is the precise thing that setting exists to switch off, so an autoplaying deck that ignores it is ignoring a direct request.

Check yourself

A "recently viewed" deck ships with six items and dots. Eight months later it holds thirty-one. What has broken?

Dots are readable at a glance up to roughly seven. Past that they degrade into a texture that says "many" — and the fix is a counter or a bar, chosen from the largest count the deck will ever hold rather than the count in the original mock.

A designer proposes making the cards fill the window exactly, removing the peek, "because the cut-off card looks unfinished". What is the strongest objection?

Discoverability, not taste. A deck with no peek and no indicator is fully functional and completely invisible — the failure mode that reports as working, because the people it fails never touch it.

A deck of full-width feature cards free-scrolls, and shows a dot for each card. What's wrong?

Snapping and indicating are the same claim made twice. If the deck names a current item, it has to be able to be on one. Free scroll is for strips of small items where landing between two costs nothing — thumbnails, tags, avatars.

Marketing wants an autoplaying hero with three slides, each carrying a headline and two lines of body copy. What do you say?

The condition autoplay cannot buy its way out of is text longer than the interval — any interval is wrong for someone. Pausing on hover, stopping on interaction and honouring reduced motion are all required too, but they mitigate a deck that already passes the content test. This one doesn't.

What you can now do

Look at any deck and say what its indicator is promising, whether the content will let it keep that promise a year from now, and what on screen tells a first-time visitor there is anything to the right of the edge. Say whether the items are pages or a strip, and hold the snap decision to that answer. And when autoplay comes up, argue it on the four conditions rather than on taste — the content test first, because it is the one that usually settles it.
