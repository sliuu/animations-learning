Lesson D009 · 17 minutes

Loading, waiting, and optimism

You almost never get to make the wait shorter. You do get to decide what is true on screen while it happens — and that decision is worth more than the milliseconds you were never going to win.

Skill: pick a waiting treatment from the length of the wait, and defend it ← Lesson D008

D008 handed you the order in which meaning arrives: you chose what the reader reads first and how long they wait for the rest. Here the clock belongs to somebody else. The network decides how long, and all you own is the story the screen tells in the meantime. That sounds like the smaller job. It is the one people quit over.

Say it out loud

Spinner — an indeterminate indicator. It says working and can never say how much longer.
Progress — a determinate indicator. It claims a proportion, which means it can be caught lying.
Skeleton — a grey stand-in with the shape of the content that is coming.
Shimmer — the sweep across a skeleton that says not real yet.
Flash of loading — a loader that appears and disappears too fast to read.
Optimistic update — showing the result before the server has confirmed it.
Rollback — taking that result back when it turns out you were wrong.
Perceived performance — how long it felt. The only number the reader has.

Four panes below, one request, one clock. Change the latency and the ranking changes with it — which is the whole reason "use a skeleton, not a spinner" is not a rule.

Try this, in order
  1. Load at 0.15s. Watch the spinner and the skeleton, not the card.
  2. Load at 0.8s, then look back at the pane marked Nothing.
  3. Load at 8s. Look away, count to five, look back — and ask which pane can tell you whether anything has happened since.
  4. At 8s again: which pane would make you reload the page?

Two facts decide the treatment

A wait has exactly two properties that matter to a designer: how long it is, and whether you know how long it is. Every choice on that stage follows from those, and neither of them is a taste question you can settle in a design review by preference.

Under roughly a quarter of a second, the correct treatment is nothing at all. You saw why: a spinner that lives for 150ms is not feedback, it is a flicker, and the eye files a flicker under something went wrong with the page rather than something is happening for me. This is the failure that gets shipped most often, because it is invisible in a design file — the mock shows the loading state as a still image, and a still image of a spinner always looks like it is doing its job.

The consequence is a rule worth saying out loud in a review: don't render the loader immediately. Start it after 300 to 500 milliseconds, and most waits never reach it — they resolve first and the reader simply sees the answer, which is what a fast interface feels like. The ones that do reach it were genuinely slow, and now the loader is telling the truth.

From about half a second to a couple of seconds, something has to acknowledge the press, and here the skeleton beats the spinner on a second job as well as the first. A spinner says working. A skeleton says working and here is the shape of what is coming, so when the content lands it fills a frame the reader has already read, instead of arriving as a jump.

Past about five seconds the indeterminate indicator stops working entirely, and it fails in a specific way rather than just getting boring. The spinner at second eight is identical to the spinner at second two — it has no way to distinguish still working from hung, so a reader with a slow connection has no evidence either way and does the rational thing, which is reload and start the whole wait again. A determinate bar is the only thing on that stage still making a claim you can check against the clock.

Which is also the catch. A determinate indicator is a promise about a proportion, and a promise can be broken in public. A bar that races to 90% and sits there is worse than no bar at all, because it made a specific claim and you watched it fail. If you cannot compute the fraction honestly, you do not have a progress bar; you have a spinner wearing a costume.

The one to remember

Choose the treatment from the length of the wait, not from the component library. Under ~250ms show nothing; to a couple of seconds show the shape of the answer; past five seconds you owe a number you can actually stand behind.

If you have read the engineering track: the 300–500ms delay before showing a loader is the same shape of argument as the one in 0001 about durations — the threshold is perceptual, not stylistic, and picking the number by feel in a mock is how it gets picked wrong.

A skeleton is a claim about layout

The reason a skeleton earns its place is that it is specific. It is not a polite grey rectangle; it is the layout of the thing you are about to receive, with the content taken out. An avatar-sized circle means an avatar is coming. Three rows of decreasing width mean a name, a line of text, and a timestamp. The reader is given a map, and a map is only useful if it matches the territory.

So the skeleton's failure mode is not ugliness, it is a broken promise about geometry. When the real content arrives at a different size, everything below it jumps — and that jump is worse than the one you would have got from a spinner, because the reader had already started reading the layout. You did not save them the shift; you made them a false map first and then shifted anyway.

Two consequences that come up constantly in review, both of them worth having a sentence ready for. A skeleton for content whose size you genuinely cannot predict — a comment thread, a search result set, anything of unknown length — is a guess presented as a promise, and one generic block that resolves into nine items is not better than a spinner. And a skeleton that resolves to an empty state is the sharpest version of the same fault: you showed the reader the shape of three results and then told them there were none.

If you have read the engineering track: 0011 is the argument for why the jump costs what it costs, and 0012 is the one about elements that appear without being announced. A skeleton is the design-level answer to both at once: it reserves the space and tells you what is going to fill it.

The two loops, and who asked for them

A spinner rotates forever. A shimmer sweeps forever. Both of them are unrequested, looping, attention-taking movement that starts without the reader doing anything — which is the precise description of what somebody is switching off when they turn on reduced motion.

The mistake is to read that as "so remove the feedback". The loop is the problem, not the indicator. Turn on reduced motion in your system settings and look at the lab above again: the spinner stops turning and the caption beside it is doing the work it was always doing, and the skeleton stops shimmering and is still, unmistakably, a skeleton. Nothing was lost that was carrying information.

The progress bar keeps moving, and that is deliberate. Its movement is not decoration applied to a state — it is the state, drawn. Freezing it would be removing information rather than removing an effect, which is a good general test for what survives that setting.

If you have read the engineering track: 0009 makes this argument in full, including why "kill all animation" is the wrong reading of the request.

The best wait is the one you never show

Everything so far has been about designing a wait well. The bigger win is not having one. If you already know what the answer will almost certainly be, you can show it immediately and reconcile with the server afterwards — the row appears the instant the reader clicks, and the network catches up in private.

This is where optimism gets sold on the happy path and the bill arrives later, so the lab below has a switch that makes the request fail.

Try this, in order
  1. Save once at 0.4s, succeeding. Then at 2s. Note how much better the right-hand pane feels as the latency grows.
  2. Now set the request to fails, still at 2s. Watch both panes at once.
  3. Count the number of times each pane's list changed during that one save.
  4. Ask yourself what the right-hand pane should do if you had already navigated away.

The left pane's cost is time and nothing else. It was slow and it was honest, and at no point did the screen contain something untrue. The right pane's cost is the third step above: for one action, the interface changed twice. It said yes, and then it took the yes back — and that second change is not an error state you bolt on afterwards, it is the feature you are actually designing when you choose optimism.

Which gives three conditions, and they hold together. Optimism is right when the action almost always succeeds, when the failure is cheap to undo, and when the reader will still be there to be told. Starring a message, adding to a list, toggling a setting: all three hold, and the interaction is transformed. Submitting a payment, sending a message to another person, confirming a booking: the first condition might hold, the second never does, and a rollback on the third one is not a rollback, it is an apology for something the reader has already told someone else about.

The last condition is the one that gets forgotten, and it is a design constraint rather than an engineering one. An optimistic update is only safe while the reader is still looking at the place where you would take it back. If the pattern lets them start the action and immediately move on — hit save, close the panel, go to another page — then the rollback has nowhere to land, and a toast that appears on a screen they have left is not a rollback at all.

The one to remember

Optimism does not remove the wait. It moves the cost from everyone, every time to the few people whose request failed — and it only pays if you have designed what those people see.

One nuance the lab shows and most products skip: the pending row is marked. A dashed edge and a hollow dot say this is here but not confirmed, and it makes the rollback legible when it comes. The cost is that you have put a small piece of visual noise in front of the 99% of readers whose save was going to work, which is a real argument for dropping it. The way to settle that in a review is to ask how bad the rollback is: quiet failure, quiet marker; expensive failure, keep the marker, or don't be optimistic at all.

If you have read D005: this is the same distinction under a finger — the difference between a gesture that is tracking and one that has been committed. A pending row is the list-shaped version of a drag that has not yet been accepted.

Check yourself

A filter re-queries a table. Most responses come back in about 120ms, but the slow tail runs to roughly a second. What should the loading treatment be?

Consistency is the trap here: showing the loader every time means most readers get a 120ms flicker, which reads as the page glitching. Delay the loader past the common case and it only ever appears when it is telling the truth. A progress bar would be claiming a proportion nobody can compute.

A search page shows a three-card skeleton while it loads. For a query with no matches, it resolves into "No results found." What is the fault?

A skeleton is specific by design; that specificity is why it beats a spinner. The same specificity means it is a promise about what is coming, and an empty state breaks it. Where the result count is unknowable, a skeleton is a guess dressed as information.

An export takes between four and ninety seconds, and the backend cannot report how far along it is. Someone proposes a progress bar "so it feels faster".

A determinate indicator is a checkable promise, and the 90%-and-hold version is the broken promise made deliberately. But a spinner is not the fallback at this length — anything that looks identical at second five and second eighty gives the reader no evidence it is alive. Named stages, or a running elapsed time, restore the one thing a long wait has to have: something that changes.

Which of these should not get an optimistic update?

Two conditions fail at once. The failure is not cheap to undo — the reader believes another person has the message — and closing the composer means the rollback has nowhere to land. The other three are cheap, reversible, and happen somewhere the reader is still standing.

What you can now do

Look at any loading state and say what the wait's two facts are — how long, and whether the length is knowable — then say whether the treatment on screen follows from them or from the component library. Catch the flash of loading before it ships, because it will not show up in a mock. Say what a skeleton is promising and whether the arriving content can keep that promise. And when optimism is proposed, take the conversation straight to the failure: how often, how expensive, and where the reader will be standing when you take it back.
