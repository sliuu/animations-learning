Lesson D005 · 17 minutes

Things you drag

While your finger is on it there is almost nothing to design — the thing has to be exactly where your hand is, and that's the whole rule. Everything a designer decides happens at the two ends.

Skill: review a drag by its pickup and its release, not its middle ← Lesson D004

A button is a request: you press it and something else happens. A drag is not a request. The thing is in your hand, and the interface has agreed to pretend that a rectangle of pixels is a physical object you have picked up. Every rule in this lesson comes out of protecting that pretence.

Say it out loud

Direct manipulation — you move the thing itself, rather than a control that represents it.
Lift — the rise on pickup: elevation, shadow, a touch of scale.
Ghost — the faint stand-in left behind where the thing came from.
Gap — the space that opens to show where it will land.
Settle — the short move out of your hand and into the slot, on an accepted drop.
Snap-back — the return when a drop is refused or abandoned.
Drop target — the region that will accept it, which has to be legible before you let go.

Start with the pretence itself. Two chips in one field. Both move with the pointer, and one of them has a CSS transition left on it. Grab either, whenever you like.

Try this, in order
  1. Drag one, then the other, then the first again. Zigzag — fast changes of direction are where it lives.
  2. Set the transition to 220 ms and do it again.
  3. Now 60 ms. Can you still tell which is which?
  4. Reveal the answer, and drag the honest one with the ring showing.

The ring is your actual pointer. On one chip the ring stays inside it; on the other the ring runs out ahead and the chip catches up afterwards. That gap is the whole failure, and it is worth naming precisely: the chip is no longer the thing you are holding. It has become a thing that is following you.

At 220 ms nobody misses it. At 60 ms most people can't name what's wrong and still behave differently — they overshoot, they move slower, they check. That is the part worth taking seriously, because 60 ms is well inside the range that gets called "smooth" by someone reading a spec rather than holding a phone.

The one to remember

Nothing under the finger gets a transition. Not an easing curve, not a hundred milliseconds, not a touch of smoothing. Interpolation is a claim about where the object is, and while someone is holding it, only they get to make that claim.

This is almost never a decision anyone made. It is what you get when a drag handler is added to a component that already had a hover transition on it, and it is what transition: all guarantees. The fix is one line — the dragged element gets transition: none for as long as it is held — and the bug is invisible in every medium except a real finger on a real screen.

If you have read the engineering track: lesson 0002 is about which properties are cheap enough to animate. This is a different rule and a stricter one — on the thing being held, the answer isn't which property, it's don't.

So where is the design?

At the edges, and there are five of them: one at pickup, two during, and two at the release — because a release has two possible endings and they need different answers. Here is a list you can reorder, with a switch for each. As in the previous lesson, switching one off never breaks it: the order always comes out right. Only what the drag was saying goes missing.

Try this, in order
  1. Drag a row two places up. Then drag one off the bottom of the list and let go.
  2. Switch Gap off and reorder again. Notice when you learn where it went.
  3. Switch Gap back on and Ghost off. Different loss — name it.
  4. Switch Snap-back off and drop one outside the list.
  5. Switch Lift off and pick something up. Did you?

Lift is the entire vocabulary of picked up, and it is borrowed from the physical world without apology: the thing rises toward you, so it casts a bigger shadow and grows very slightly. Two per cent of scale is plenty. Its job is to answer the question the hand asks first — am I holding this yet, or am I about to scroll the page?

Gap and ghost answer two different questions and most implementations only answer one. The gap says this is where it will land. The ghost says this is where it came from, and where it goes back to if you change your mind. Showing both at once costs one row of reserved space and is the more generous choice in any list long enough that the origin can scroll away from you.

If you have read the engineering track: the gap opening is a layout animation of the kind 0011 is about, and it should be built the same way — four rows each changing one transform. Nothing here animates height or margin.

Settle is the accepted ending. The row leaves your hand and travels into the slot it has been showing you, in about two hundred milliseconds, and it lands — no bounce. A bounce here is the most common way a competent drag ends up feeling like a toy, because overshoot implies momentum and there was none: you put it down, you didn't throw it.

Snap-back is the refused ending, and it is the one that gets skipped, on the grounds that people don't often drop things in the wrong place. They do, and the moment they do is the moment they most need an answer. Watch what happens with it switched off: the row is in your hand, you let go, and it is somehow back in the list with nothing joining the two. The first question is not did it move? It is did I just delete something?

If you have read the engineering track: 0008 is worth re-reading against this. A spring is right when the release carries velocity — a flung card, a sheet you shove downward. A row you put into a slot has none, and a spring will invent some.

Nobody can drag with a keyboard

Drag is the least accessible interaction pattern in wide use, and the fix is not an attribute. There is no ARIA that makes a pointer gesture available to someone who doesn't have a pointer. The only fix is a second path to the same outcome, and it is usually about ten lines: focus a row in the lab and press ↑ or ↓.

Worth being honest about who that serves, because "keyboard" undersells it. It serves someone with a tremor, someone using a switch or voice control, someone on a phone on a moving bus, and someone who simply finds a five-slot drag more fiddly than two key presses. A drag with no alternative path is a feature that quietly excludes people who have no way to report why they stopped using it.

The review question: what is the non-drag path?

A kanban board's cards have transition: all 150ms ease on them, added originally for the hover shadow. What does that do to the drag?

The interpolation is a claim about where the card is, and it contradicts the hand. The dragged element needs transition: none while it is held — the transition belongs to the moment after the release, not before it.

A reorderable list shows a gap at the landing position but leaves no mark where the dragged item came from. Which question is unanswered?

Gap and ghost are two promises, not one. In a short list you can hold the origin in your head; in a long one it has scrolled away, and abandoning the drag becomes a guess.

A designer specs the drop as a spring with a small overshoot, "so it feels physical". The interaction is dropping a row into a slot in a settings list. Why is that likely wrong?

Springs are right where the release carries velocity — a flung card, a sheet shoved downward. Borrowed physics has to match the physics the hand just performed, or it reads as a toy.

A team is asked how their drag-to-reorder works for keyboard users and answers that the list has full ARIA roles and labels. What is missing?

Labelling a gesture does not make it performable. Arrow keys on a focused row is the usual answer, it is a handful of lines, and it also serves people with tremor, people using switch or voice control, and anyone on a moving bus.

What you can now do

Review a drag in three passes instead of one. Is the held thing exactly under the hand — no transition, no smoothing, no lag. Are both during-questions answered — where will it land, where did it come from. Do both endings exist — the settle and the snap-back, not just the happy one. Then ask what happens without a pointer. Four questions, none of which require a word about easing.
