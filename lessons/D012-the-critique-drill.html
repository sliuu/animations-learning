Lesson D012 · 25 minutes

The critique drill

Ten animations, one flaw each. Name how it feels and what to change, and only then find out. This is where the vocabulary stops being a list and becomes something you can say in a room.

Skill: look at an animation and say what is wrong with it in one sentence someone can act on ← Lesson D011

D011 ended with a way to say no: an animation is worth its cost or it isn't, and the cost is its length times how often it plays. That gets you through a review where the question is should this exist. It does not get you through the far more common one, where the animation should exist, is going to ship, and is subtly wrong — and the only thing between it and production is whether you can say what's wrong before the meeting ends.

Say it out loud

A critique in three parts — the flaw (how it feels, in one word), the fix (the change to make, in one clause), the principle (why that fix, in one sentence). All three, or it isn't one.
A hedge — "it feels a bit off to me." True, unarguable, and unusable: there is nothing in it for the other person to do or to disagree with.
An adjective — mushy, floaty, twitchy, sluggish, busy, disorienting, unanchored, gratuitous. Each names a specific defect with a specific repair, so the wrong one sends the fix in the wrong direction.

They are deliberately not defined yet. You get them by feeling them, one specimen at a time, and the table that separates them is at the bottom of this page where it belongs.

How to use the drill

Run the left pane twice before you answer, and answer from the feeling rather than from the description underneath — the description is there so the drill still works with motion turned off, not as a hint. Commit even when you are unsure; a wrong word you committed to teaches you more than a right word you hedged into. Once you have committed, both panes replay in the same instant, which is the only honest way to compare two things that differ in how they feel.

The adjectives, now that you have felt them

Every word below names a different defect and — this is the part that makes them worth learning — a different repair. Reaching for the wrong word does not just describe the problem imprecisely; it sends the other person off to fix the wrong thing.

WordWhat it actually meansThe repair it asks for
Mushy Feedback to something the reader is physically doing arrives late. Almost always a symmetric curve on a press. Make the response half immediate. The recovery half can stay slow.
Sluggish Correct in every respect except that it takes too long. The most over-diagnosed word on the list. Cut the duration — or, for a group, cut the offset between items.
Floaty No sense of weight or arrival. Usually a long symmetric curve, or a scale that starts from nothing. Ease out, start near the final size, and land it in about 200ms.
Twitchy Too much motion for how easily it is triggered. Hover is where this lives, because hover is triggered by accident. Shrink the distance and the duration, and remove any overshoot.
Busy Too many things claiming to have changed at once. A failure of quantity, not of pace. Remove claims. Speeding a busy animation up produces a fast mess.
Disorienting The reader lost track of where something went, or where it came from. A spatial failure. Give it a consistent off-screen home, and enter from there.
Unanchored Arrived from nowhere in particular, so there is no implied way back. The signature of a bare fade on a panel. Enter from the edge it will leave by, or grow it from the control that opened it.
Gratuitous Nothing changed. The motion is not reporting, guiding or confirming anything at all. Delete it. The only word here whose repair is removal.
Janky Dropped frames. A rendering fault rather than a design one — and the one word here you should check before using. Not a design note. Hand it over with a recording, not an adjective.

Saying it in a room

The three-part form is not a script for sounding rigorous. It is a filter that catches your own bad notes before you give them, because a note that cannot produce all three parts is usually a note about your taste.

Try it against the hedges. "It feels a bit off" is a flaw and nothing else — no fix, no principle, so the other person's only move is to guess. "Can we make it faster?" is all fix: it might be right, but with no flaw named you cannot tell whether faster addresses the actual problem, and half the time it doesn't — busy gets faster and stays busy. "Motion should always be purposeful" is all principle, which is the worst of the three, because it is unarguable and applies to everything, so it lands as a verdict on the designer rather than as a note on the work.

It fails in the other direction too. A flaw with no fix is a complaint. A fix with no principle is an instruction, and it will be followed exactly once — the next screen will have the same problem, because nobody learned anything transferable. The principle is the part that makes the note worth the meeting.

The form, filled in

Flaw: "The menu feels unanchored."
Fix: "Slide it in from the right edge instead of fading it up."
Principle: "The direction it enters from is the promise about where it goes when it closes — a fade doesn't make one, so there's no implied way back."

Thirty-five words, and every part of it is arguable. That is the point: the other person can now tell you the menu is deliberately centred, or that the right edge is already taken, and you have a conversation instead of a standoff.

The engineering track argues most of these from the mechanism, if you have read it: 0001 for floaty, 0002 for busy, 0010 for mushy and twitchy, 0009 for what gratuitous costs a reader who has asked for less motion.

The one you should not give

"Janky" is on the vocabulary list because you will hear it, not because you should say it. It means dropped frames — a specific, measurable rendering fault — and it is the one word here that a designer cannot verify by looking. Everything else you can settle by watching the thing twice. This one needs a recording, and the difference between an animation that drops frames and one that is merely floaty is invisible to the naked eye and enormous to whoever has to fix it.

So when something feels wrong in a way none of the other eight words fit: say what you saw, hand over the case, and let it be measured. "It stutters on my machine when the sidebar is open" is a better note than "it's janky", because it is a note someone else can reproduce.

Lesson 0014 is the reading-a-recording lesson, for being the person who settles it rather than the person who reports it.

Check yourself

A row of eight filter chips. Every chip fades and lifts in when the filter panel opens, each one 90ms after the last. The panel is opened dozens of times a day.

The offset is the fault, not the animation. Eight chips at 90ms apart means the last one has not begun until well after the reader has read the first, and this plays dozens of times a day. Busy is the tempting answer, but a stagger is one claim made in sequence rather than eight claims at once — a group arriving is a single event.

A toggle switch. On tap, the knob slides across in 260ms with a spring that overshoots slightly before settling into the far end of the track.

Not every specimen is broken, and the reflex to find a flaw is itself worth drilling against. The knob travels a real distance to a real new position, the overshoot is a physical claim that a sliding object plausibly makes, and 260ms for a state change is ordinary. Mushy would apply if the knob waited before starting. It does not.

An engineer shows you a modal that scales up from the centre of the screen over 500ms on an ease-in-out curve. You have ten seconds to give one note.

Only the first has all three parts inside one sentence: the flaw is floaty, the fix is the two numbers, and the principle rides on "ease-out" — a thing arrives, it does not accelerate into place. The second is a fix with no flaw. The third is a principle with no note in it. The fourth is a defensible fix that leaves the curve, which is the actual defect, untouched.

A search field shows a spinner while results load. Results usually come back in about 100ms, and the spinner appears and disappears within that time.

The two repairs point in opposite directions and only one is right by default. A delay means fast responses show nothing at all. A minimum display time fixes the flicker by making every fast response artificially slow — the right tool only for cases that survive the delay and then finish just after it. Delay first.

You tell a designer their card hover is twitchy. They ask what to change, and you have to pick the repair that actually follows from the word you used.

Twitchy is a mismatch between how much motion plays and how easily it is triggered, so the repair is always to shrink the motion. The delay is the trap: it stops the accidental triggers, but it also makes the deliberate ones feel unresponsive, which trades a twitchy hover for a mushy one.

What you have now

Nine words, each attached to a specimen you have actually watched and a repair that follows from it. That is the whole trick. Vocabulary is worth building not for precision's own sake but because "mushy" and "sluggish" send the fix to two different places, and saying the wrong one costs a round trip that a design review does not have time for.

The drill is re-runnable, and it is worth re-running cold in a month. The specimens will not have changed, but the thing being tested is whether the word arrives before the explanation does — and that is the only measure of whether the vocabulary is yours yet.
