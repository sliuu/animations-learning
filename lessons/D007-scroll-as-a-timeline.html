Lesson D007 · 20 minutes

Scroll as a timeline

A scroll system is choreography for an audience that controls the clock, can reverse it, and may leave at any moment. The design is not the effect. It is how much of that clock each idea is allowed to spend.

Skill: review a scroll system by who owns each frame, how much distance a beat costs, and whether the page keeps the native scroll contract ← Lesson D006

D006 asked whether a deck's indicator could keep its promise as the content grew. A scroll system makes a larger promise: move your page, and I will move with you. It has to keep that promise whether the reader crawls, flicks, reverses, stops halfway, or skips the section in one gesture.

Say it out loud

Scroll choreography — arranging page changes against the reader's travel.
Beat — one idea with a beginning, a change, and a resting point.
Trigger — crossing a threshold starts a time-based animation.
Scrub — scroll position owns animation progress, frame for frame.
Pin — hold one visual in place while the surrounding story advances.
Parallax — layers move at different rates to claim depth.
Horizontal section — vertical travel drives a sideways sequence.
Scroll-jacking — the page rewrites the distance, direction, or response of the reader's input.

In the lab, do not start by making it beautiful. Start by deciding what kind of content it is, then make scroll the wrong kind of clock for it on purpose.

Try this, in order
  1. Leave the content on an entrance and scroll on starts each beat. Drag the little scrollport yourself, then use Stop halfway.
  2. Change only scroll to owns every frame. Stop halfway again. The casualty is on screen: a half-made headline whose reading is conditional on another gesture.
  3. Change the content to an explanation. The same scrub now earns its place, because the middle frame explains how separate signals become a pattern. Reverse it.
  4. Try half a screen, one screen, and two screens per beat. Use Scroll it for me so your attention is free for the pace.
  5. Let the visual travel away, then turn the depth layers on. Read the verdict after each: pinning and parallax are claims, not polish switches.

First question: must the middle frame mean something?

A trigger and a scrub can produce the same first frame and the same last frame. They disagree about every frame between. With a trigger, scroll says begin and then gets out of the way. Time finishes the movement even if the reader stops. With a scrub, scroll never gets out of the way: stop the page at 43%, and the animation must live at 43% indefinitely.

That gives you the review question: is 43% a valid picture? For a decorative entrance it usually is not. Half a clipped headline, half an opacity fade, or a person frozen midway through a gesture is merely unfinished. Trigger it. Let it arrive. For an explanation — an exploded object assembling, a route drawing, before becoming after — 43% may be the entire point. Scrub it, because the reader is inspecting the relationship between states rather than waiting for an entrance to end.

This is why trigger for product UI, scrub for storytelling is a useful smell and a bad rule. A hero entrance on an expressive page still wants a trigger. A data relationship inside a product can still deserve a scrub. The content of the middle frame decides, not the family the page belongs to.

If you have read the engineering track: 0004 teaches the mechanism as fire versus track. The design test is the sentence above it: stop at 43% and ask whether the frame communicates something complete. If it does not, the mechanism is already chosen before anyone opens an API.

Distance is duration

A time-based animation asks for milliseconds. A scroll-driven one asks for distance. The reader supplies the speed, so 500 milliseconds is not a thing the design can promise; half a viewport, one viewport, or two viewports per beat is.

A beat is one idea the page asks the reader to notice before it changes the subject. Give three beats half a screen each and a trackpad flick can consume the whole argument as one visual blur. Give a sentence two screens and the page starts charging admission for a thought that would have taken one line in normal flow. Both are pacing failures. One is rushed; the other is indulgent.

One viewport per beat is a useful starting point, not a law. Shorten it when the states are obvious and cumulative. Lengthen it when the middle is the content — an assembly, a comparison, a camera move whose spatial relationship needs time to be inspected. Then run the two adversarial gestures: cross the whole section in one fast flick, and creep backwards through it. Every beat still needs a legible resting point, and reverse order still needs to make sense.

The one to remember

On a scroll timeline, distance is duration. Price every beat in viewport-heights, then ask whether the idea is worth what the reader has to move to receive it.

Pinning spends the page on one comparison

Pinning holds a visual still while copy, labels, or states advance around it. It is right when the same object must survive across several beats so the reader can compare what changed. A phone that keeps its position while three features appear on its screen; a map that stays put while three routes are explained; a material that remains visible as its layers are peeled apart. The still object is the common noun in three changing sentences.

Turn the pin off in the lab and the later copy keeps talking about a picture that has already left. That is the honest failure. The reverse failure is more common in polished work: pinning a visual that never changes, then asking the reader to scroll through three screens of captions beside it. Nothing is being compared. The pin has turned ordinary reading into a toll booth.

Use this test: what must remain visually constant so the change is understandable? If you can name it, pin that. If the answer is nothing, let the page flow. Pinning is not a way to make a section important; it is a way to hold the control variable still.

Parallax is an adjective: deep

Parallax means layers move at different rates. The slower layer reads as farther away; the faster one reads as nearer. It is not merely motion in three places. It is a claim that the scene has depth.

That claim can clarify a hero made of foreground type, a product plane, and a background field. It can also make a flat layout feel like its cards have slipped off their grid. The knob in the lab is deliberately restrained: a few pixels are enough to make the planes disagree. If the content has no foreground/background relationship, the correct setting is one plane. Motion is the loudest hierarchy channel; three layers moving at three rates spend it three times.

A horizontal section is the same kind of claim about structure. Vertical scroll can drive a sideways sequence when the content is genuinely ordered left-to-right — a process, a timeline, chapters laid on one rail. Keep the page's vertical progress visible, keep a clear resting state at each chapter, and keep the section short enough that a reader always knows how to leave it. A horizontal translation does not become a deck just because cards move sideways: in a deck the hand moves the strip; here the hand still moves the page and the page maps that travel onto a stage.

If you have read the engineering track: D003's hierarchy argument applies here without being required background. Parallax does not decorate three layers equally; it ranks them by apparent distance, and the fastest plane will usually win your eye before the largest type does.

The native contract

There is a line between using scroll as a timeline and replacing scrolling with your own interpretation of it. Scroll-jacking is crossing that line: changing how far an input moves the page, changing its direction without a visible reason, trapping it inside a scene, or making the viewport ease toward the place the reader already asked to be.

The last one often arrives under the name smooth scrolling. Smooth means the viewport trails the input and catches up later. It can look luxurious in a screen recording because a recording has no hand in it. Under a real trackpad it feels laggy: the visible page is late relative to the gesture that caused it. That word is sensory, so here is the exact definition: laggy means the object you are controlling remains behind the position your hand already requested.

Try this, blind
  1. Put the pointer over either pane and make one quick wheel or trackpad gesture. Both panes receive the same distance.
  2. Stop sharply. The small dot is the requested position; watch which viewport is still travelling towards it.
  3. Use Wheel it for me if you want to watch rather than perform the input.
  4. Commit to the pane that feels attached, then press Which is which?. The sides shuffle on load, so no letter in this lesson can give the answer away.

The trailing pane is not broken. It is a faithful version of a widely shipped technique: the input updates a destination, and an eased animation chases it. That is why the comparison matters. The case against scroll-jacking is not that its implementation is crude. It is that the implementation changes ownership. The reader issued a command and the page treated it as a suggestion.

There are defensible exceptions: a bounded story where the mapping itself communicates the idea, an installation meant to be watched rather than navigated, a product demonstration with a clear skip. But a normal page is not an installation. It contains links, reading, search, selection, the browser's back button, and a person who may simply be trying to reach the footer. Native scroll is the baseline because all of those behaviours already agree on it.

Reduced motion means changing the composition

Scroll effects are unusually important to reduce because they can occupy a large area, last for the entire journey down a page, and respond continuously to an interaction the reader cannot avoid. The accommodation is not to run the same camera move faster. Flatten the parallax. Stop scaling and rotating large planes. Let each beat be present in normal flow, or replace a scrubbed transformation with a short triggered cross-fade.

Keep the information. A progress bar can still report position. A pinned comparison can become a vertical stack whose captions sit beside their images. A horizontal chapter can become an ordinary list. Reduced motion is a different composition of the same argument, not a blank page and not a frozen first frame.

If you have read the engineering track: 0009 makes the accessibility decision element by element. For scroll systems, the seam is especially clean: keep status and sequence, remove simulated camera travel and depth. Both labs here keep the motion because it is the evidence being studied, and neither one moves until you ask.

The CSS behind it

The design decisions do not belong to one library. Native CSS can bind an animation to the nearest scroll container; JavaScript and timeline libraries can calculate the same 0–1 progress. The receipt is the same mental model either way:

Show the mechanism 
/* A scrub: position, not elapsed time, owns progress. */
@keyframes assemble {
  from { transform: translateX(-20%); opacity: .2; }
  to   { transform: translateX(0);    opacity: 1; }
}

.piece {
  animation: assemble linear both;
  animation-timeline: view(block);
  animation-range: entry 20% cover 55%;
}

/* A trigger: crossing the line adds a class; time finishes. */
.line { transition: transform 420ms var(--ease-out-strong); }
.line.is-in { transform: translateY(0); }

Notice what is absent from that code: pinning, beat distance, and the reason for parallax. Those are layout and editorial decisions. CSS can make a sticky element and a timeline library can expose a pin option; neither can tell you whether the same visual must survive across three ideas. Tools implement the contract. They do not choose it.

Check yourself

A hero headline reveals as the reader enters the section. If the reader stops halfway, half the letters remain clipped indefinitely. What is the strongest fix?

The content is an entrance, not an explanation. A half-made word is not a useful state, so scroll should choose when the reveal starts and then give ownership to a short time-based animation that finishes even if scrolling stops.

A three-stage exploded product diagram needs readers to inspect how the parts join and reverse to compare an earlier state. Which driver fits?

The middle is the content. Scroll owns every frame so the reader can inspect, pause and reverse the assembly. The designer's job is then to give each stage enough distance and a clear resting point.

A pinned section spends five viewport-heights showing one static product image while five short captions replace each other. What should the review ask first?

Pinning earns its distance by holding a control variable still across changing states. A visual that never changes and is not needed for comparison is not a control variable; it is a toll booth placed in front of ordinary reading.

A site applies a polished 700ms catch-up to every wheel and trackpad input. The motion is fluid and never drops a frame. Why can it still feel wrong?

A beautiful interpolation can still violate the interaction. Native scroll treats distance as a command; catch-up scrolling treats it as a destination the page may reach later. The lag, not dropped frames or the curve, is the design failure.

What you can now do

Look at any expressive page and name its beats. For each one, say whether scroll merely starts it or owns every frame, and defend that answer by stopping in the middle. Price the beat in viewport-heights. Name what a pin is holding constant, what depth a parallax layer is claiming, and what tells a reader how to leave a horizontal section. Then test the native contract with one abrupt gesture: if the viewport keeps moving after the hand has stopped, say exactly what was taken away.

I'm your teacher here — ask me things. Good asks for this one: “take a scroll section from a site I like and mark its beats”, “show the same story as a trigger and a scrub in production CSS”, “how would I spec a pinned horizontal section in Figma”, or “open the Performance panel and tell me whether this parallax is paying for paint”.
