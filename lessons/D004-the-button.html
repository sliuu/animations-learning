Lesson D004 · 16 minutes

The button

Not one animation. Seven states, each of which answers a different question the user is holding — and the one everybody argues about in review is the least important of them.

Skill: review a control by what it promises, state by state ← Lesson D003

A button is the smallest complete piece of interaction design in software, and it is the only one you can hold entirely in your head. Everything a bigger flow has to do — say it is available, say it heard you, say it is working, say it worked — a button has to do too, inside a hundred pixels and about two seconds.

Say it out loud

State — a condition a control can be in. A button has about seven, not one.
Affordance — the signal that something can be acted on, given before anyone touches it.
Acknowledgement — the reply that says heard you, sent before the result exists.
Pending — the stretch where the system has your instruction and no answer yet.
Optimistic — showing the result before it is confirmed, and handling being wrong later.
Scrub — progress tied 1:1 to something the user is doing, and can reverse.
Snap-back — the return when a gesture is abandoned or refused.

Here is one save button, wired up properly, with a switch for each state. Switching a state off doesn't break the button — the click still saves, every time. It only removes what the button says while that is happening.

Try this, in order
  1. Click each switch once and watch the button: every switch plays its own state, so you can see all five without having to catch them.
  2. Click them all back on. Press the button as it comes, with a real mouse-down, and read the five lines beside it.
  3. Switch Press off — the switch plays the moment again and there is nothing in it. Now click the button for real. Notice how long you wait before you believe anything happened.
  4. Switch Pending off too and click. Now do the thing you just felt like doing.
  5. Switch Success off. Everything works. Nothing was ever confirmed.

Every one of those states is answering a question, and the questions arrive in order. Can I press this, and is it the one I mean? — hover. Where am I? — focus. Did that land? — press. Is it working, or is it stuck? — pending. Did it work? — success. A button with a beautiful hover and no press state has answered the least urgent question on the list and skipped the most urgent one.

Press is the one that gets cut, and it gets cut because its absence is invisible in every medium a design gets reviewed in. It isn't in the mockup. It isn't in the spec. It isn't in the screenshot in the pull request. The only place it shows up is under a finger, and the only symptom is that people click twice.

The one to remember

The press state is the only feedback you can give in under a hundred milliseconds, because it is the only one that doesn't have to wait for an answer. Everything else on the screen is at the mercy of the network. That one is free, and it is the one that stops the second click.

If you have read the engineering track: lesson 0010 is the mechanics of this — how far to scale, why down and not up, why the release is slower than the press. This is the reason to bother: the press state is not a flourish, it is the receipt.

Nothing is a state, and it is the worst one

Run the lab with pending switched off and watch the nine hundred milliseconds in the middle. The screen is not doing something inadequate; it is doing nothing at all, and it is identical to the screen from before the click. Which means the user cannot distinguish working from the click missed from this app is broken — three very different situations rendered as the same pixels.

The job of a pending state isn't really to entertain someone for a second. It is to make the button inert — unresponsive on purpose — while keeping it legible. Those two have to happen together, and the common mistake is to get inert by reaching for disabled, which greys the label out. Grey means you can't do this. You want I am doing this. Same inertness, opposite sentence.

Which is the honest version. The other option is optimistic: show the result immediately, before the server has agreed, and roll it back if you were wrong. A heart that fills the instant you tap it is optimistic — and it is the right call, because it is nearly always right and the cost of being wrong is a heart quietly emptying again. A payment button is not, because the cost of being wrong is a person who believes they have paid. The rule is not about how long the request takes. It is about what a retraction would cost.

Disabled is usually an error message with the text removed

The seventh state is the one the lab doesn't switch, because it deserves an argument rather than a toggle. A disabled button is doing two things: refusing, and refusing to say why. The reason exists — a required field is empty, the plan doesn't include this, you already used your three — and the design has decided to hold it back until the user guesses.

The alternative is nearly always better and nearly never done: leave the button live, and spend the press on the explanation. They press Publish, nothing publishes, and the empty field it needs gets flagged and focused. That is one extra state and one fewer dead end, and it works on a touchscreen, where the tooltip strategy has never worked at all.

Keep disabled for the case where the reason is already visible on screen and nobody could miss it — the empty cart, the unticked terms box directly above. If you have to write a tooltip to explain the disabled state, the tooltip is the admission that the button should not have been disabled.

The state you are inside

Every state so far is momentary: the button decides what to say and says it. There is one more kind, and it changes the shape of the problem — the state the user is inside, and can leave.

Hold-to-confirm is the smallest example there is. Progress is scrubbed by the finger, at the finger's pace, and it can be abandoned at any point. Three knobs here, and all three are design decisions rather than tuning: how long you make somebody commit for, whether you show them the commitment, and what letting go early means.

Try this, in order
  1. Hold it to the end at 1100 ms. Then hold it and let go at about halfway.
  2. Set progress to nothing and try to delete the project. It still works perfectly.
  3. Back to a fill. Set 2500 ms and hold it all the way. Sit with how long that is.
  4. Set early release to it keeps the progress and let go twice in a row.

With the indicator off, the button is functionally perfect and unusable, which is a cleaner proof than an argument: the progress fill is not decoration on the interaction, it is the interaction. Without it there is no way to know the hold is working, no way to know how much is left, and no reason to keep holding. A gesture with no progress feedback is a gesture nobody completes.

And the release is where the design actually lives. Letting go at 40% has to snap back, visibly and quickly — the user needs to see the thing they escaped from being undone. Keeping the progress feels reasonable in a spec and is quietly horrible in the hand: the button is now armed, and nothing told you. The snap-back also runs shorter than the hold that earned it, on purpose. A return is a dismissal, not a rewind, and it shouldn't charge you again for the time you already spent.

If you have read the engineering track: this is 0008's argument arriving from the design side. Once the user is inside the state, duration stops being the unit you think in — the finger sets the pace, and the only thing you get to time is what happens after they let go.

Two-and-a-half seconds is worth sitting with because it ships. Hold-to-confirm is a good pattern when the action is destructive and frequent — a dialog every time would be worse than the risk. It is a bad pattern when the action is destructive and rare: nobody discovers it, they tap, nothing happens, and they conclude the button is broken. Its real cost is that it has to be learned, and you only get to spend that once.

A team ships a button with a considered hover, a clean focus ring and a spinner while the request is in flight — but no press state. What is the predictable symptom?

The spinner cannot appear until the request starts, and the request cannot start until the click has already gone unanswered for a moment. The press state is the only feedback that doesn't depend on anything.

A Send invoice button is set to update optimistically: the row shows Sent the instant it is clicked, and reverts if the request fails. What is wrong with that choice?

The question is never how long the request takes; it is what a retraction costs. A like reverting is a shrug. An invoice un-sending is a phone call.

A Publish button is disabled until three fields are filled, and a tooltip on hover explains which. Why is this still a bad design?

Leave the button live and spend the press: flag and focus the first missing field. Same refusal, with the reason attached, and it works on a phone.

In a hold-to-confirm control, letting go at 60% leaves the progress fill where it is rather than returning it. What has that state promised?

Abandoning a gesture should look like abandoning it. The snap-back is how the user sees that they got out, and on a destructive action that reassurance is the entire point of the pattern.

What you can now do

Review a control by walking its states out loud instead of reacting to its animation. Name the question each one answers, and find the one that isn't answered — it will usually be press, pending, or the reason behind a disabled. The sentence that gets you there is not I think the button needs more polish. It is: what does this button say between the click and the response?
