Lesson D010 · 20 minutes

Navigation and page transitions

A transition between two screens is a sentence about how they are related. You are writing that sentence whether or not you meant to — and the reader reads it either way.

Skill: choose a transition model from the relationship between two screens, and make the way back mean something ← Lesson D009

D009 was about the wait: something is happening, and the screen has to say so honestly. This is the other half of the same moment. The request came back, the new screen is here, and the reader has a different question now — not is it working but where am I. That question has a deadline of about a third of a second, and if the screen does not answer it, the reader answers it themselves by reading everything again.

Say it out loud

Page transition — the motion between two screens. It is a claim about their relationship, not a flourish.
Orientation — the reader's sense of where they are and how they got here.
Cut — no transition. The honest zero, and often correct.
Cross-fade — one dissolves into the other. Says different thing, says nothing about where.
Push — the new screen enters from an edge and displaces the old one. Says direction.
Shared element — one object persists across the change and moves to its new home. Says this specific thing.
Persistent chrome — the parts that do not take part: the bar, the tabs, the header.
Reversibility — whether going back looks like the undoing of going forward.
Cold entry — arriving straight at a screen from a link, a refresh or a bookmark, with no transition at all.

Four frames below. Same app, same tap, same 420 milliseconds — four different sentences about what just happened.

Try this, in order
  1. Press Navigate all four and watch only the first two. Say what each one told you. One of them told you nothing; decide whether that was a loss.
  2. Now watch only Push and Shared element. Both tell you something — say what is different about the two things they told you.
  3. Set Direction to back. Two frames change and two do not.
  4. Set Back behaviour to repeats forward. Watch the right-hand two and say, out loud, what the screen just claimed.
  5. Go back to mirrors forward and press back again, to feel the difference land.

Four models, four claims

A cut claims nothing. That is not a failure state — it is the right choice more often than transition-enthusiasts admit, particularly for navigations the reader performs dozens of times an hour. Motion has a cost that gets paid on every repetition, and a cut costs nothing. If you cannot say what the transition is telling the reader, the cut is telling them the same amount, faster.

A cross-fade claims a different thing, in no particular place. It is the mildest possible statement: something changed, be calm about it. It is genuinely useful when two screens have no spatial relationship worth asserting — a dashboard and a settings page do not live next to each other in any sense the reader can hold — and it is the safest default when you are not sure. Its weakness is exactly its virtue. It cannot distinguish going in from coming out, so it can never help the reader retrace their steps.

A push claims direction. The old screen is not gone, it is over there, one step behind you. This is the model that builds a mental map, and it is the reason phone operating systems have leaned on it for fifteen years: after a few minutes with it, the reader knows there is a stack and knows which way is out. The cost is that a push commits you. Once forward means "from the right", every navigation in the product has to have an opinion about which direction it is, and any screen that genuinely has no direction will feel arbitrary when you give it one.

A shared element claims this specific one. Of the four it is the only one that says something about the object rather than about the screens. The reader tapped a row; the thumbnail from that row is now the header of the page they are on; the identity is established without a word of copy. That is a lot of meaning for one moving square, which is why it is the model people over-reach with. It only works when the thing that persists is genuinely the same thing. Fly an arbitrary rectangle across the screen because the geometry happens to work and you have made a claim the reader will test and find false.

Then notice what did not move — the bar across the top is the same bar, and it never blinks. Now press your browser's back button and watch the thumbnail go home. Do it twice: once watching the object, once watching everything except the object. All three links checked 2026-09-05. Sites get redesigned; if one has, the exercise is still to name what replaced it.

Back is not forward

Here is the failure you will ship if nobody stops you, and it is worth dwelling on because it is almost invisible in review. Forward and back are the same navigation in the code — one screen replaced by another — so they run the same transition, and the transition plays the same way. Set Back behaviour to repeats forward in the lab and press back: the list slides in from the right, exactly as the detail did. The screen has just told the reader they went deeper, at the precise moment they were trying to get out.

The reason this survives review is that it is invisible in every artifact a review uses. A static mock cannot show it. A screenshot of a correct back and a broken back are the same image. Even a recording will pass if the person recording it only went forward. It is caught by one thing: somebody navigating in both directions, on purpose, watching. Put that in your review checklist as a sentence — press back and describe what you were told — because it will not occur to anyone spontaneously.

The fix is a direction of travel, not a second animation. If forward is "in from the right", back is the same motion with the sign flipped, and both fall out of one variable. The same is true of a shared element: forward the object flies to its new home, back it flies to the row it came from. What makes the pair legible is that they are visibly the same motion undone, which is also why you should resist the temptation to give back its own charming treatment. Back's job is to be recognisably the reverse. Charm is forward's job.

Reversibility also decides how much you can spend. A transition the reader will only see once can be expressive. A transition they will see going both ways, thirty times a session, has to survive repetition in both directions — and things that survive repetition are short, quiet, and the same every time.

If you have read 0005: this is that lesson's asymmetry argument at the scale of a whole screen. An exit is allowed to be faster than an entrance; what it is not allowed to be is a second entrance pointing the wrong way.

Go and look Chrome’s paginated view-transitions demo — a real page-to-page navigation, not a single-page imitation of one. Click through two or three pages and then use the browser back button rather than the on-page link. The motion reverses, and it reverses because the page asked which kind of navigation this was before choosing a direction. Then reload in the middle: the reload is treated as a third case and gets neither direction, which is the detail worth stealing.

Orientation is what did not move

Everything so far has been about the parts that move. The next lab is about the parts that refuse to, and this is the half that is consistently underrated: a reader knows they are still in the same app because the bar across the top is the same bar it was a second ago, not because something slid nicely.

Try this, in order
  1. Press Navigate both a few times with the default setting and watch only the left frame. Then only the right one.
  2. Set What stays put to nothing. The two frames become the same design — read the number in the corner.
  3. Set it to the tab bar, and watch the highlight rather than the content. Say what the highlight is doing that it was not doing a moment ago.
  4. Set it to the tab bar + header and read the number again.
  5. Now use Go to to jump two tabs at once, and watch the highlight travel.

The number in that lab is measured off the frame, and it is a blunt instrument on purpose. What it makes obvious is that both panes ran the same fade for the same duration — the difference in how settled the right-hand one feels was not bought with better motion. It was bought by excluding things from the motion.

Persistent chrome does three jobs at once. It answers am I still in the same place without the reader having to check. It gives the moving parts a frame to move inside, which is what stops a transition reading as the whole world being replaced. And it lets small things carry the change: when the tab bar survives, the highlight can travel to the new tab instead of reappearing on it, and that one moving pill does more orientation work than a full-page slide because it is a continuous object rather than a new arrangement.

There is a design decision hiding in that, which is worth making explicitly rather than discovering: what is the smallest region that has to change? Most navigations change less than the implementation changes. If only the content area is genuinely different, animating the whole viewport is not a bolder choice, it is a less accurate one.

If you have read D003: persistent chrome is the hierarchy argument turned inside out. That lesson asked which element should move first; this one asks which should be exempt, and the answer comes from the same ranking.

Arriving cold

Every transition in this lesson assumes the reader came from somewhere. A large share of them did not. They followed a link from a message, they refreshed, they restored a tab from three days ago, they hit a bookmark. There was no previous screen, so there was no transition, and every sentence you carefully wrote was never delivered.

This is the test that keeps transition design honest. Load the destination cold and ask whether it still explains itself: does it say what it is, does it offer a way up that is a real destination rather than "back", and does it survive having no history behind it? If the answer is no, the motion was carrying information — and motion is the worst possible place to keep information, because it is the first thing that disappears. It is absent on cold entry, absent when the reader looks away mid-navigation, absent on a slow device that drops the whole animation, and absent by request for anyone who has turned it off.

The rule that falls out is short: a transition may reinforce a relationship the screen already states. It may not be the only place that relationship exists. A shared element that establishes "this is the kettle you tapped" is doing lovely work on top of a page whose heading says Kettle. It is doing load-bearing work — badly — on a page that expected the animation to introduce it.

Go and look Chrome’s stack-navigator demo — a messages app where the transition asserts depth: screens slide over each other and back out again. Go two levels in, then copy the URL, open it in a new tab and look at what you land on. You get the same screen with none of the sentence, and the question to ask is whether the page still tells you where you are. Then go back one level and watch how the screen underneath was waiting there the whole time rather than being rebuilt.

When motion is off

Turn reduced motion on and all four models in the first lab collapse to a cut. That is the accommodation, and it is also the cleanest possible version of the cold-entry test: everything the transition was telling the reader has just gone. What is left has to answer the question by itself.

Notice what the second lab does under the same setting. It keeps working. Nothing animates, the frames swap instantly, and the right-hand one is still noticeably more settled than the left — because persistence was never motion in the first place. It is a fact about the layout. That is why it survives the setting, the refresh, the deep link and the dropped frame, and it is the strongest argument in this lesson for spending your effort on what stays rather than on what moves.

There is one honest exception worth keeping. A cross-fade is opacity only — no travel, no parallax, nothing for the vestibular system to object to — so it is a reasonable thing to keep when a reader has asked for less motion, where a push is not. If you want a graceful degradation rather than a hard cut, that is the one to reach for.

If you have read 0009: the same stance, one scale up. The distinction that lesson draws between decorative and load-bearing motion is exactly the cold-entry test above, arrived at from the other direction.

The CSS behind it

Show the mechanism
/* Browsers now do this natively, across a real page navigation. */
@view-transition { navigation: auto; }

/* Name the things that persist or travel. A name must be unique
   per snapshot: two elements with the same name in one frame and
   the transition is skipped entirely. */
.site-header { view-transition-name: header; }
.card--active img { view-transition-name: hero; }

/* Back is not forward. The type is set per navigation, and the
   direction falls out of one variable rather than two animations. */
html:active-view-transition-type(forwards) {
  &::view-transition-old(content) { animation: slide-out-left 300ms both; }
  &::view-transition-new(content) { animation: slide-in-right 300ms both; }
}
html:active-view-transition-type(backwards) {
  &::view-transition-old(content) { animation: slide-out-right 300ms both; }
  &::view-transition-new(content) { animation: slide-in-left 300ms both; }
}

/* The reader asked for less. A cross-fade is opacity only, so it
   is the honest thing to keep when travel is not welcome. */
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  ::view-transition-group(*) { animation-duration: 1ms; }
  ::view-transition-old(root),
  ::view-transition-new(root) { animation: fade 120ms both; }
}

Check yourself

A drill-in navigation pushes the detail screen in from the right. The team ships it. What is the most likely defect, and how would you find it?

Forward and back are one code path, so they get one animation unless somebody insists otherwise. Nothing in a mock, a screenshot or a forward-only recording shows it, which is why "press back and say what it claimed" belongs in the review checklist as an explicit step rather than as something a careful person would notice.

A gallery flies the tapped photo up into the detail view. On a slow connection the detail's full-size image has not arrived yet, so the transition animates the empty frame it will occupy.

The whole value of a shared element is the identity claim, and the claim is only true if what travels is recognisably the thing that was tapped. The low-resolution thumbnail is already on screen and already correct; use it, and let the full image replace it quietly on arrival. A spinner inside the flying frame advertises the gap rather than closing it.

A settings screen opens from a gear icon in the top-right corner. Which model does the most useful work?

All three of the first answers can be right; what makes them right is the relationship, not the trigger. If settings is somewhere you go, a push is honest. If it is a layer you put on top and dismiss, it should arrive as one and be dismissible as one. The defect to avoid is a screen that arrives as a place from one entry point and as a layer from another, because the reader then has no reliable way out.

Two tabs at the same level of the hierarchy. Someone proposes a horizontal push between them, in the direction of the tab order.

A push does not inherently mean "deeper"; it means "there is an arrangement, and you moved within it". Tabs have exactly that arrangement, and it is on screen. That is also the condition: if the bar goes with the content, the reader is left with a direction and nothing to be directed relative to.

A page's heading is a plain "Details", and the reader is expected to know which item they are looking at because the thumbnail flew up from the row they tapped.

This is the cold-entry test. A transition may reinforce a relationship the screen already states; it may not be the only place that relationship exists. Making the animation slower does not help, because the problem is the cases where there is no animation at all — which is a large share of arrivals at any given screen.

A redesign replaces a full-page cross-fade with one where the top bar and the side navigation stay put and only the content area changes. The animation itself is unchanged — same fade, same duration. Readers report it feels faster.

Perceived speed here is a reading cost, not a rendering cost. A full-page swap asks the reader to re-establish the whole screen; holding the frame still means only the part that actually changed has to be taken in. It might also render more cheaply, but that is not what they felt, and shortening the fade would not reproduce it.

What you can now do

Look at any two screens in a product and say what the transition between them claims — nothing, a difference, a direction, or an object — and whether that claim is true of the relationship. Say which of the four models the navigation deserves, and be willing to argue for the cut. Check the way back as a separate design, in both labs' sense: press it, describe what you were told, and refuse a back that reads as a second forward. Name the persistent chrome in any design you are shown, and notice when there is none. And run the cold-entry test on every screen a transition introduces, because that is where you find out whether the motion was decorating the information or holding it.

Ask yourself: take the last navigation you designed or built and write its sentence in words — "you went into this specific thing", "you moved sideways within a set", "something different is here now". Then load the destination cold, with no history, and see how much of that sentence is still being said.
