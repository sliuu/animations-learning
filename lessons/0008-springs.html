Lesson 0008 · 20 minutes

Springs, and what a duration can't express

Seven lessons of "how long, and what curve." Here's the case that model can't describe — and the three numbers that replace it.

Skill: choose between a duration and a physics model, and tune a spring without guessing ← Lesson 0007

Every animation you've written has answered the question how long? A spring refuses to be asked.

Duration-and-curve carries a hidden assumption: that at the moment the animation starts you already know where it's going, and that nothing will change its mind on the way. For most UI that's true, which is why the model has served you fine for seven lessons.

It stops being true the moment a human is holding the thing. Drags, flings, sheets that can be caught mid-flight, a toggle clicked twice in 200ms — in all of those the target changes while the element is moving, and a function of time has no way to represent that.

Try this, in order
  1. Hit Move a couple of times. The two lanes are roughly comparable — this is the case where the choice doesn't matter.
  2. Hit Interrupt mid-flight. Watch the grey dot at the moment it turns around, then watch the same moment in the graph.
  3. Look at velocity carried in the readout.
  4. Press bouncy, then no bounce, and watch settles in change — a number you never typed.

What you just saw

At the interruption, the grey trace has a corner and the orange one has a curve. That corner is a velocity discontinuity, and it's the entire lesson.

A tween is a function of time. Give it a start value, an end value, a duration and a curve, and its position at any instant is fully determined. Change the target mid-flight and there is nothing to do but throw the old function away and start a new one from wherever the element happens to be — and the new function's opening velocity comes from the curve, not from what the element was doing a millisecond ago. With ease-in-out it stops dead and starts again. With a strong ease-out it yanks straight into a fast reversal. Both are corners. Neither has any memory.

A spring is a function of state — position and velocity. Changing the target changes one term in a force equation that is still being solved, and the velocity term is untouched. The dot keeps moving the way it was already moving and curves into the new direction, the way a thrown object does. That's what the readout is telling you: the spring carried real velocity across the change; the tween carried none.

The rule this gives you

A tween is a function of time. A spring is a function of state.

If the target can change while the thing is moving, you want state. If it can't, the extra machinery buys you nothing.

Notice what you never typed: a duration. The settles in number is derived — the lab simulates the spring offline until it stops moving and reports how long that took. With a spring, duration is an output. That is the source of both its power and most of its annoyance.

The three numbers

A spring isn't a curve you pick, it's a rubber band you describe. Three properties describe it, and the motion is whatever falls out.

NumberPhysicallyTurn it up and…
stiffnessHow hard the band pulls toward the target Everything gets faster and more urgent
dampingFriction — resistance to movement Bounce disappears; too much and it crawls
massInertia — resistance to changing speed It feels heavier, starts slower, overshoots further

Those three interact, which is why tuning by dragging sliders feels like guesswork. It isn't, though — they collapse into one number that predicts the feel almost completely.

The damping ratio, which is the number that actually matters

ζ = damping ÷ (2 × √(stiffness × mass))

The lab prints it. It answers one question — does this overshoot, and by how much? — and that question is most of what a reader perceives.

ζCalledBehaviour
< 1underdampedOvershoots the target and comes back. Bounce.
= 1critically dampedThe fastest possible arrival with no overshoot.
> 1overdampedNo overshoot, and slower than it needs to be. Sluggish.

Critically damped is a genuinely useful landmark: it's the boundary. Below it you're buying bounce; above it you're buying nothing at all. If you don't want bounce, aim at ζ ≈ 1 rather than cranking damping up, or you'll ship something that feels like it's moving through honey.

Two things worth noticing. Bouncy is the slowest — bounce is not free, it's paid for in time. And gentle and snappy have almost the same ζ; the difference between them is stiffness alone. That's the practical tuning method: set ζ for how much bounce you want, then use stiffness for speed.

Settling time is not perceived duration

A spring approaches its target asymptotically — mathematically it never quite arrives, so "settles in 733ms" is really "gets within a rounding error in 733ms." The visible motion is over well before that. The tail is real in the numbers and invisible on screen.

This is the single most confusing thing about springs in practice, and it's why Motion added a second way to describe one. Instead of stiffness and damping, you can give it visualDuration — how long it should look like it takes — and bounce, from 0 (none) to 1 (extremely bouncy).

// Physics-first: precise, and hard to reason about.
{ type: 'spring', stiffness: 420, damping: 30, mass: 1 }

// Perception-first: the same idea, in units you can actually judge.
{ type: 'spring', visualDuration: 0.35, bounce: 0.2 }

Reach for the second one. You'll tune it in a third of the time, and the two axes are genuinely independent: visualDuration sets the stiffness, bounce sets ζ, and mass is pinned to 1 so it stops being a knob at all. That's the tuning method from the previous section — set ζ for bounce, then stiffness for speed — with the axes already pulled apart for you. Keep the physics parameters for when you're matching something specific.

bounce is ζ, inverted

These are not the same number, but they're one subtraction apart. Straight out of Motion's source, in both the duration and visualDuration paths:

ζ = 1 − bounce          // clamped to [0.05, 1]

So bounce: 0 is ζ = 1 — the critically damped row above. bounce: 0.3, the default, is ζ = 0.7. bounce: 1 is ζ = 0.05, which is very nearly frictionless and looks it. The lab's presets convert the same way: no bounce → 0, gentle → 0.21, snappy → 0.27, heavy → 0.42, bouncy → 0.66.

Two consequences worth carrying around.

You can't express overdamped. ζ > 1 is off the map — every bounce of 0 or below clamps to ζ = 1, so the third row of the ζ table is unreachable through this API. That's a deliberate omission rather than an oversight, given that the verdict on overdamped was "slower for no benefit." But if you ever want deliberate sludge, you need the physics parameters.

bounce is linear in ζ, and ζ is nowhere near linear in what you see. The first overshoot of a step response is exp(−πζ ÷ √(1 − ζ²)), which falls off a cliff:

Springs in CSS, honestly

CSS has no spring. What it has is linear(), an easing function that takes an arbitrary list of output values instead of four control points — so you can hand the browser the shape of a spring, sampled.

The lab generates it for whatever spring you've dialled in; it's in the box at the bottom. Values above 1 are how the overshoot survives:

transition-duration: 629ms;
transition-timing-function: linear(
  0, 0.073, 0.223, 0.396, 0.561, 0.703, 0.816, 0.898, 0.949,
  0.988, 1.01, 1.021, 1.025, 1.024, 1.02, 1.017, 1.013,
  1.009, 1.006, 1.003, 1.002, 1.001, 1
);
The catch, and it's the whole lesson again

That's a baked spring. Sampling the physics turns it back into a function of time — which means it looks like a spring and interrupts like a tween.

So linear() is right for one-shot decorative motion where you want the character of a spring with no dependency. It is not right for the case that made you want a spring in the first place. If you need velocity continuity, you need a real solver, which means JavaScript.

When to use one

Yes: anything the user can grab. Drag-and-release, sheets and drawers, swipe-to-dismiss, a knob on a toggle, carousel snapping. Anything whose target can change mid-flight. Anything that should feel like it has physical weight.

No — and these matter more:

  • Orchestration. Lesson 0003's arithmetic — total = duration + (n−1) × step — needs a duration. Springs don't have one, so a spring-driven stagger has no predictable total length. Use tweens for anything choreographed.
  • Exits. Lesson 0005: get out of the way, fast, and don't come back. An overshoot on the way out is an element changing its mind about leaving. Tween exits, even when the entrance is a spring — the asymmetry is correct.
  • Anywhere overshoot would be a lie. A progress bar can't go past 100%. A panel that overshoots past a container edge exposes whatever's behind it. A counter that springs past its value and back is telling the reader something false for 200ms.

Overshoot is not decoration — it's a claim that the thing has mass. That's true of a card you flung and false of a dropdown menu.

Three mistakes

Bounce on everything. There's a phase everyone goes through where the whole interface is made of jelly. The tell is bounce above about 0.3 on things that aren't being directly manipulated. Menus, tooltips and modals should almost always be ζ ≈ 1 or a plain tween.

Springing opacity. Opacity clamps at 1, so the overshoot is invisible — you get none of the character and all of the long tail, which reads as a fade that's mysteriously slow to finish. Spring the transform, tween the opacity alongside it at a short fixed duration. That pairing is what most good spring animations actually are.

Overshoot into a clip. If the parent has overflow: hidden, or the element sits flush against an edge, the overshoot is either invisible or ugly. Check the container before you buy the bounce.

Reduced motion

Springs are the worst offender here, and it's worth being blunt about it. Oscillation is precisely the kind of movement that triggers vestibular symptoms — more so than travel distance, which is what most prefers-reduced-motion handling worries about.

The fix is not a stiffer spring. Under reduced motion, replace it with a short tween or a plain fade. bounce: 0 is a compromise nobody asked for; a 120ms fade is an answer.

What can a spring express that a duration-and-curve cannot?

A tween can absolutely overshoot — that's what a back or elastic curve is. What it can't do is carry the velocity it already had into a new target, because it's a function of time with no memory of state. That's the corner you saw in the graph.

You want a spring with no bounce at all. What do you do?

ζ = 1 is critical damping: the fastest arrival with zero overshoot. Going past it is overdamped — still no bounce, but slower for no benefit, which is what "cranking damping to the maximum" gets you. Lowering stiffness just makes it sluggish while keeping the bounce.

Why is a spring a poor choice for a staggered list entrance?

Lesson 0003's budgeting — total length, overlap ratio — needs a known duration per item. A spring's duration is an output, and an asymptotic one at that, so you can't budget with it. Choreography wants tweens.

You paste the lab's generated linear() curve into a CSS transition. What have you got?

Sampling the physics into a list of values turns it back into a function of time. Overshoot survives — that's what the values above 1 are — but velocity continuity doesn't, because there's no solver left, only a curve. Fine for one-shot motion; useless for the interruptible case.

A sheet springs up from the bottom of the screen. What should its opacity do?

Opacity clamps at 1, so a spring's overshoot is invisible on it and you're left with only the long asymptotic tail — a fade that seems slow to finish for no visible reason. Spring the transform, tween the opacity. That pairing is most good spring animation.
