Lesson 0001 · 12 minutes

Ease-out, or nothing

Why motion exists on the web, and the one dial that separates expensive-feeling from amateur.

Skill: choose a duration and a curve, and defend both Reference 01

Screens teleport. Brains don't.

Every object you have ever tracked with your eyes travelled through the space between where it was and where it ended up. Your visual system is built on that assumption — it is how you know the thing that arrived is the same thing that left. A screen breaks the assumption constantly: a menu that was not there is now there, fully formed, and you have to work out where it came from.

That work is small, but it is real, and it is the thing animation exists to remove.

First principle

Animation's job is to preserve cause and effect. It answers questions the user would otherwise have to answer themselves: where did that come from, where did it go, did my click register, is this the same thing I was just looking at.

So the test for any animation is one question: does this reduce the work of understanding what just happened, or add to it? Everything below is a corollary of that.

This gives you a sharp definition of bad. Bad animation is not ugly animation — it is animation that adds work. It makes you wait. It moves things you were not asking about. It draws your eye to the wrong place. A page where everything animates is a page where nothing is emphasised, because emphasis is a contrast effect and contrast needs something still to push against.

And it gives you a definition of good that has nothing to do with taste: good animation is animation you do not notice, that leaves you feeling the interface was quick.

The gate

Before the how, the whether. The deciding variable is not how nice the animation is — it's how often the user will see it.

FrequencyVerdict
100+ times a day (⌘K, arrow keys)None. Ever.
Tens of times a day (hovers, tabs)Cut it, or 100–150ms
Occasional (modals, toasts)Standard treatment
Rare or once (onboarding, marketing)Room for delight

The logic is the first principle again. A first encounter with a modal is a moment of genuine confusion — where did this come from — and motion resolves it. The four-hundredth time you hit ⌘K, there is no confusion left to resolve. All that remains is the wait. Delight has a half-life; friction does not.

Sometimes the best animation is no animation. Emil Kowalski, You don't need animations

Now feel the difference

Three dots. Identical duration — the slider proves it. The only variable is the curve. Hit Race and watch which one you believe is fastest.

They arrive together. They always did. But ease-in feels slower than ease-out, and at 600ms it feels almost broken — like the page is thinking before it responds.

Read the little graphs to see why. Horizontal is time, vertical is progress. The playhead traces the curve as it plays.

  • ease-in hugs the bottom at the start: time is passing, nothing is happening. That dead window sits exactly where the user's attention is highest — the instant after they acted.
  • ease-out leaps up immediately, then flattens. The user gets confirmation in the first few frames, and the tail end — where nothing important is left to communicate — is where the time gets spent.
  • linear is a straight diagonal, and reads as mechanical. Nothing with mass starts and stops instantaneously.
  • ease-out-strong is the same shape as ease-out, pushed harder. This is the difference between motion that feels intentional and motion that feels like a default, and it is why the CSS keywords are rarely the right final answer.
Try this

Drag the duration to 150ms and race again. Every curve becomes acceptable — at short durations easing barely matters. Now drag to 1200ms. ease-in is unbearable and ease-out is merely slow. Duration is the volume knob; easing is the tone. Long durations are what expose a bad curve.

So: entrances and exits get ease-out. Things responding to a user get ease-out. This is not a stylistic preference — it is front-loading the information into the window where the user is looking.

The full decision tree — including when ease-in-out and linear are right — is in Reference 01. Ease-in has essentially no place in UI.

Build the canonical entrance

This is the fade-and-rise you have seen on every well-made marketing page, including the one that started this. Edit the CSS; the preview updates as you type. Replay re-mounts the element so the entrance runs again.

Three things in that snippet are doing real work, and each is a rule:

1. Opacity and transform, and nothing else

These two are the only properties the browser can hand entirely to the GPU. It composites them without recalculating layout or repainting anything. Animate height, margin, or top instead and the browser redoes layout every single frame — for the animated element and potentially everything around it. That is where jank comes from. Comeau's cost hierarchy: height/width/left are expensive, background-color is middling, transform/opacity are nearly free.

2. Named properties, never all

transition: all 400ms works today and betrays you in three months, when someone adds a background-color to the card and it now lazily fades on hover for reasons nobody can find. Name what you mean.

3. 24px, not 200px

Set the translate to 200px in the playground. It stops reading as this element is arriving and starts reading as this element is flying in from somewhere. The motion should be just enough to imply the element came from somewhere — a suggestion of travel, not a journey. The Claude page uses 10px, 20px and 24px, and nothing larger.

The mistake to see once

Two cards. One starts at scale(0), one at scale(0.95). Replay a few times and watch the left one.

The left card is a magic trick; the right card is an object arriving. The difference is five hundredths of a unit of scale.

Why it matters is the first principle again: scale(0) has no size, so there is no object for your visual system to track — it has to accept that something materialised. A deflated balloon still has a shape. Starting from 0.95 gives the eye something to follow the whole way, so the arrival reads as continuous rather than as a cut.

Somewhere between 0.95 and 0.4 it stops working. Find that point yourself — that is the kind of judgement that does not transfer by being told.

GSAP's power scale is just named curve strength — power1=Quad through power4=Quint, per the GSAP docs. So power2.out ≈ cubic-bezier(0.215, 0.61, 0.355, 1), and power4.out is the same shape as the --ease-out-strong you just raced. Reference 01 has the full translation table — that table is your reverse-engineering tool.

The real lesson

A polished page is not using a secret library. It is applying a handful of rules consistently, in whatever tool is at hand. Consistency is most of what reads as expensive.

Retrieval

Close the reading above before answering. Getting one wrong here is more useful than getting it right by scrolling up.

A command palette opens on ⌘K, dozens of times a day. It currently scales in over 200ms. What should change?

Keyboard-initiated, high-frequency. There is no confusion left to resolve after the tenth use, so the motion is pure latency. Raycast ships with no open animation for exactly this reason.

Two entrances run at exactly 400ms, one ease-in and one ease-out. Why does ease-in feel slower?

The dead window at the start lands precisely where attention is highest — right after the user acted. Ease-out spends its slow part at the end, where there is nothing left to communicate.

A drawer opens over 300ms. What duration should its close use?

Exits go faster than entrances. Entering, the user is being shown something; leaving, they have already decided and want it gone. Symmetrical timing makes dismissal feel like it is resisting you.

A card grid animates in from transform: scale(0). What is the core problem?

At scale 0 there is no object for the eye to track, so the arrival reads as a cut rather than as motion. Start at 0.95 with opacity 0 — a deflated balloon still has a shape.
